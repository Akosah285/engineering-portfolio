## Birthday paradox simulator

Drag the room size and watch the collision probability climb past 50% near

<Explainer demoTitle="Birthday paradox simulator">
  <Step>
    <p>
      How many strangers do you need in a room before two of them share a
      birthday with 50% odds? Your gut probably says hundreds — there are
      365 days, after all. The actual answer is 23. The point of this demo
      is not the trivia; it is to feel <em>why</em> intuition fails so
      badly here.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The trap is asking the wrong question. "What is the chance someone
      shares <em>my</em> birthday?" grows linearly in <em>n</em>. The
      paradox asks "what is the chance <em>any</em> two of us share a
      birthday?" — that compares all
      <MathExpression latex="\binom{n}{2} = \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}" />
      pairs at once, and pairs grow quadratically.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Easier to count the complement: probability all <em>n</em> birthdays
      are distinct. The first person picks any day. The second avoids one
      day, giving <MathExpression latex="(D-1)/D" />. The third avoids two,
      giving <MathExpression latex="(D-2)/D" />. Multiply them all:
    </p>
    <p>
      <MathExpression display latex="P(\text{no clash}) = \prod_{k=1}^{n-1} \left(1 - \tfrac{k}{D}\right)" />
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The collision probability is just 1 minus that product. The demo
      computes it in log-space (<MathExpression latex="\log(1-k/D)" />
      summed, then exponentiated) so it stays numerically stable for large
      <em>n</em> and large <em>D</em> — naive multiplication of 200 small
      numbers would underflow to zero on you.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      For a closed-form intuition, use
      <MathExpression latex="\log(1-x) \approx -x" /> on each factor and
      sum: the exponent collapses to
      <MathExpression latex="-n(n-1)/(2D)" />. That gives the textbook
      approximation
      <MathExpression display latex="P(\text{collision}) \approx 1 - e^{-n(n-1)/(2D)}" />
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Set that equal to <MathExpression latex="\tfrac{1}{2}" /> and solve
      for <em>n</em>: you get <MathExpression latex="n \approx \sqrt{2D \ln 2} \approx 1.177\sqrt{D}" />.
      For <MathExpression latex="D = 365" /> this is 22.49, so the smallest
      integer that does the job is 23. The exact and approximate curves in
      the chart agree to within a percent past <em>n</em> = 10.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      This <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{D}" /> threshold is the real
      headline. Whenever you have <em>D</em> equally-likely outcomes and
      you sample with replacement, you start seeing collisions after about
      <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{D}" /> samples — not <em>D</em>, not
      <em>D</em>/2. That square-root is what makes the paradox feel like
      magic.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Switch to the "Hash collision (D=256)" preset. The threshold drops to
      about <em>n</em> = 19, since <MathExpression latex="1.177\sqrt{256} \approx 18.8" />.
      That is also why a "128-bit" hash is only safe to about
      <MathExpression latex="2^{64}" /> trials against birthday attacks —
      the bit-budget halves once your adversary is hunting for any
      collision, not a specific preimage.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      A subtle modelling assumption: birthdays are uniformly distributed
      over the year. They are not — real human birthdays cluster in
      September and bunch around weekday inductions. The true probabilities
      land slightly higher than the uniform model. Whenever you read "the
      birthday paradox in real data," that is the gap being patched.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The featured problem <em>"Birthday paradox — why 23 people gives a
      50% collision probability"</em> just below walks the algebra in full,
      including the
      <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{2 \ln 2}" /> constant. For other rare-
      event counting tricks where the indicator-variable shortcut beats
      brute enumeration, see the coupon collector featured problem in the
      same section.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Stationary distribution of a 3-state Markov chain via the eigenvalue 1"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 27, FA19."
>
  <p>
    Given a row-stochastic transition matrix
    <MathExpression latex="P" /> for a three-state ergodic chain, find
    the stationary distribution <MathExpression latex="\pi" /> — the
    long-run fraction of time spent in each state — without simulating
    the chain.
  </p>
  <p>
    By definition <MathExpression latex="\pi" /> is the left eigenvector
    of <MathExpression latex="P" /> with eigenvalue 1, normalised to a
    probability vector:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\pi P = \pi, \qquad \sum_i \pi_i = 1"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Take the concrete weather chain from the Markov-chain visualizer
    above (sun → rain → cloud transition probabilities):
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.5 & 0.2 \\ 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Writing out <MathExpression latex="\pi P = \pi" /> gives three
    equations (only two are independent) plus the normalisation
    <MathExpression latex="\pi_1+\pi_2+\pi_3 = 1" />. Solving:
    <MathExpression latex="\pi_1 = 0.485" />,
    <MathExpression latex="\pi_2 = 0.297" />,
    <MathExpression latex="\pi_3 = 0.218" /> (rounded).
  </p>
  <p>
    Two checks: the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees a unique
    positive stationary distribution for an irreducible aperiodic chain,
    and you can verify by iterating <MathExpression latex="P^n" /> for
    any starting row — it converges to a matrix whose rows are exactly
    <MathExpression latex="\pi" />. Watch the demo: no matter which
    state you start in, the time-averaged occupancy approaches these
    numbers. This is the bridge from "Markov chain as random walk" to
    "Markov chain as linear operator on the simplex" — the eigenvalue
    structure is the dynamics.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Base-rate fallacy: a 99%-accurate test for a 1%-prevalent disease"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 27, FA19."
>
  <p>
    A disease has prevalence <MathExpression latex="P(D) = 0.01" /> in
    the screened population. A test has sensitivity
    <MathExpression latex="P(+\!\mid D) = 0.99" /> and specificity
    <MathExpression latex="P(-\!\mid \neg D) = 0.99" />. You test
    positive. What is the probability you actually have the disease?
  </p>
  <p>
    Bayes' rule, written for clarity:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid D)\, P(D)}{P(+ \mid D)\, P(D) + P(+ \mid \neg D)\, P(\neg D)}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Plug in the numbers:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.01}{0.99 \times 0.01 + 0.01 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0198} = 0.5"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Half. A "99%-accurate" positive on a disease that only 1% of the
    population has gives you a coin flip. The intuition trap is that
    "99% accuracy" sounds like the answer should be near 99%, but the
    likelihood ratio
    <MathExpression latex="P(+\!\mid D)/P(+\!\mid \neg D) = 99" /> only
    multiplies the prior odds. Prior odds were 1:99, so posterior odds
    are 99:99 = 1:1.
  </p>
  <p>
    Drop the prevalence to 0.1% and the same test gives
    <MathExpression latex="P(D\mid +) \approx 0.09" />. This is exactly
    why screening guidelines target high-risk subgroups: shifting the
    prior by an order of magnitude shifts the posterior by an order of
    magnitude. The Bayes' theorem visualizer above lets you slide the
    prior + sensitivity + specificity and watch the posterior move; it's
    the most direct illustration I know of why "base rate matters" is
    one of the highest-leverage habits this course gave me.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Coupon collector — n·H_n draws via the indicator-variable trick"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 27, FA19."
>
  <p>
    A cereal box contains one of <MathExpression latex="n" /> equally
    likely coupons. On average, how many boxes must you buy to collect
    the full set? Naïve intuition says "around <MathExpression latex="n" />",
    but the actual answer is
    <MathExpression latex="n H_n \approx n \ln n + \gamma n" />, which
    grows noticeably faster.
  </p>
  <p>
    Decomposition is the trick. Let
    <MathExpression latex="T_k" /> be the additional draws needed to
    pick up a new coupon once you already hold
    <MathExpression latex="k - 1" /> distinct types. Each draw is
    novel with probability
    <MathExpression latex="(n - k + 1)/n" />, so
    <MathExpression latex="T_k" /> is geometric with mean
    <MathExpression latex="n / (n - k + 1)" />. Total draws:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="T = T_1 + T_2 + \cdots + T_n,\qquad \mathbb{E}[T] = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{n}{n - k + 1} = n \sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{1}{j} = n H_n"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Linearity of expectation collapses the sum without ever needing
    independence (although in this problem the
    <MathExpression latex="T_k" /> are in fact independent). For
    <MathExpression latex="n = 100" />, the expected wait is about
    518 draws (versus 100 if every draw magically produced a new
    coupon); for <MathExpression latex="n = 1000" /> it's about 7485.
  </p>
  <p>
    The variance is <MathExpression latex="\pi^2 n^2 / 6" /> to
    leading order, so the typical fluctuation is on the same order as
    the mean — coupon collection is a high-variance process. Monte
    Carlo it on a few thousand trials (the random-process visualizer
    above is the same indicator-variable pattern in motion) and the
    histogram is conspicuously right-skewed, with a long tail driven
    by the always-painful hunt for the last coupon. The result
    generalises immediately: any time you're sampling-with-replacement
    until you cover a set, expect a
    <MathExpression latex="\log n" /> blow-up relative to the
    sampling-without-replacement baseline.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Birthday paradox — why 23 people gives a 50% collision probability"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 27, FA19."
>
  <p>
    Pick <MathExpression latex="k" /> people uniformly at random from
    a year with <MathExpression latex="n = 365" /> equally likely
    birthdays. What is the probability that at least two share a
    birthday? Most people guess "you'd need ~180 people to get to
    50/50". The real answer is <strong>23</strong>, and the gap
    between intuition and arithmetic is the whole pedagogical payoff.
  </p>
  <p>
    Solve the complement. Let <MathExpression latex="A_k" /> be the
    event "all <MathExpression latex="k" /> birthdays distinct". Lay
    the people out one at a time: person 1 lands anywhere, person 2
    must avoid one date, person 3 must avoid two, and so on, giving
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\Pr[A_k] = \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} \frac{n - j}{n} = \frac{n!}{(n-k)! \, n^{k}}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    For small <MathExpression latex="k/n" /> the product is well
    approximated by
    <MathExpression latex="\exp\!\left(-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} j/n\right) = \exp\!\left(-k(k-1)/(2n)\right)" />.
    Setting that equal to <MathExpression latex="1/2" /> and solving
    gives the famous <MathExpression latex="k \approx \sqrt{2 n \ln 2} \approx 22.49" />.
  </p>
  <p>
    Plugging the exact formula in: at
    <MathExpression latex="k = 23" /> the collision probability is
    50.7%; at <MathExpression latex="k = 30" /> it is 70.6%; at
    <MathExpression latex="k = 50" /> it is 97.0%. The deeper lesson
    travels well beyond birthdays: any time you sample
    <MathExpression latex="k" /> items uniformly from
    <MathExpression latex="n" /> bins, expect a collision once
    <MathExpression latex="k = O(\sqrt{n})" />. That is exactly the
    <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{n}" /> "square-root attack" on
    hash functions and the reason a 256-bit hash gives 128-bit
    collision resistance — the same combinatorial accounting,
    re-skinned for cryptography.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>
