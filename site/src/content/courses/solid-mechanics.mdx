<Explainer demoTitle="Method of joints">
  <Step>
    <p>
      Picture the metal triangles you can see inside a railway bridge,
      or the crisscrossing bars above the ceiling of an old gymnasium.
      Those are trusses — structures that carry surprisingly large loads
      using only thin, straight members. The trick is geometric: arrange
      the bars into triangles, bolt them together at the corners, and
      the whole lattice becomes stiff. This demo lets you load any joint
      of a few classic trusses and watch every bar's internal force fall
      out of one tidy calculation.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      A truss has two strong assumptions baked in. First, every joint is
      a frictionless pin — it transmits force but not moment. Second,
      all loads are applied <em>only</em> at those joints, never along a
      bar's length. Those two together mean every member is loaded only
      by an axial pull or push from each end: no bending, no shear
      inside a bar — just a single number per member, tension if
      positive and compression if negative. That is the whole aha — the
      most intimidating-looking lattice collapses to one scalar per bar.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Load the <strong>Simple Triangle</strong> preset: joints A(0,0),
      B(4,0), C(2,2), with a pin at A, a y-roller at B, and a 4 kN
      downward load at the apex C. By symmetry the vertical reactions
      are 2 kN up at each support. Walk to joint C and write force
      balance — <MathExpression latex="\sum F_x = 0" /> and
      <MathExpression latex="\sum F_y = 0" /> — for the two diagonals
      AC and BC plus the applied load. Both diagonals share the same
      axial force by symmetry; they pull C downward and outward to add
      to zero with the 4 kN load.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The two diagonals come out the same:
      <MathExpression latex="F_{AC} = F_{BC} \approx -2.83 \text{ kN}" />,
      i.e. each is in compression carrying roughly 2.83 kN. Now drop to
      joint A: the bottom chord AB pulls A rightward with tension
      <MathExpression latex="F_{AB}" />, while AC pushes A leftward and
      downward. Horizontal balance gives
      <MathExpression latex="F_{AB} = +2 \text{ kN}" /> — a tension.
      The bottom chord is stretching outward to stop the apex from
      spreading the supports apart, which is exactly what you would
      guess by squeezing a paper triangle flat on the table.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Now generalise. At each of J joints you write two scalar
      equations, one for x and one for y, giving 2J equations in total.
      Your unknowns are the M member forces plus R reaction components
      from the supports — a pin contributes 2 reactions, a roller 1.
      The system is exactly solvable when
      <MathExpression latex="M + R = 2J" />. That is what statics
      textbooks call <em>statically determinate</em> — translated, you
      have exactly as many equations as unknowns, so there is one
      answer and no guesswork about material stiffness.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Add a member without adding a joint and you have more unknowns
      than equations: the truss is statically <em>indeterminate</em>,
      and equilibrium alone cannot pin down the forces — you would need
      material stiffness and a deformation-compatibility argument too.
      Take a member away and the count tips the other way: too few
      unknowns, the system is unsolvable, and the geometry usually
      flops into a mechanism. This demo's solver checks the count up
      front and surfaces an "unsolvable" badge instead of returning
      nonsense.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Doing every joint by hand the way we did C and A works for a
      triangle, but the <strong>Warren Truss</strong> preset already
      has five joints and seven members — chasing the algebra by hand
      gets tedious fast. The demo instead stacks every joint's two
      equilibrium equations into one matrix system
      <MathExpression latex="A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}" />, where
      <MathExpression latex="A" /> is a 2J × (M+R) geometry matrix of
      direction cosines and <MathExpression latex="\mathbf{x}" />
      packs the unknown member forces and reactions. LU decomposition
      solves it in one shot.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Some bars carry no force at all under a given load. They are
      still there because they stabilise the truss under different
      load cases, but for this load they do nothing. Two giveaway
      patterns: a joint with only two non-collinear members and no
      load applied to it, or a joint with three members where two are
      collinear. Toggle <strong>Show zero-force members</strong> on
      the Warren preset and you will see a diagonal or two quietly
      drop to zero — the geometry is doing the work without them.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Switch to the <strong>Cantilever</strong> preset and slide the
      load between joints J3 and J4. The top chord flips between
      tension and compression as the load moves above or below the
      neutral line — exactly the same sign change you will see later
      when you reach <strong>Bending stress distribution</strong> and
      watch <MathExpression latex="\sigma = My/I" /> change sign across
      the neutral axis. A truss is doing in discrete bars what a beam
      does in continuous fibres.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      So that is the method of joints: the simplest statics problem
      where geometry alone — once you commit to pin-joints and
      joint-only loads — gives you every internal force. The very next
      demo, <strong>Beam deflection calculator</strong>, drops the
      pin-joint assumption and lets bars bend, which is where moment,
      curvature, and the flexural rigidity
      <MathExpression latex="EI" /> enter the story. Read them
      back-to-back: trusses count axial-only forces in discrete bars;
      beams generalise the same equilibrium logic to a continuous
      member that resists bending too.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

## Mohr's circle

Drag the stress element; watch the Mohr's circle and principal stresses update.

<Explainer demoTitle="Mohr's circle">
  <Step>
    <p>
      Pull a bolt straight up and you know the tensile stress — load over
      area, end of story. But what is actually happening on a slanted
      plane cut through the bolt at thirty degrees? Hidden inside the
      rod is a different combination of normal-and-shear stress on every
      possible angle, and those combinations are what crack bolts, snap
      drive shafts, and bring down beams. Mohr's circle is the single
      picture that shows all of them at once.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Take a concrete stress state at one point in a body:
      <MathExpression latex="\sigma_x = 80" /> MPa pulling the element
      rightwards, <MathExpression latex="\sigma_y = 20" /> MPa pulling
      it upwards, and a shear of <MathExpression latex="\tau_{xy} = 30" />
      MPa twisting the faces. Three numbers describe the stress
      completely. We want to know what σ and τ a tiny face would feel
      if we rotated that element to some other orientation. The
      brute-force way is to plug angles into the transformation
      equations. Mohr's trick is to plot, not plug.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Plot the σ-axis horizontally and the τ-axis downwards. The centre
      of the circle sits at the average normal stress,
      <MathExpression latex="C = (\sigma_x + \sigma_y)/2 = 50" /> MPa,
      always on the σ-axis. The radius comes from the half-difference
      and the shear,
      <MathExpression latex="R = \sqrt{((\sigma_x - \sigma_y)/2)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2} = \sqrt{30^2 + 30^2} \approx 42.4" />
      MPa. That single circle, centre (50, 0) and radius 42.4, contains
      every (σ', τ') pair this point can ever feel — one circle, all
      orientations.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Mark the x-face at
      <MathExpression latex="(\sigma_x, \tau_{xy}) = (80, 30)" /> and
      the y-face at
      <MathExpression latex="(\sigma_y, -\tau_{xy}) = (20, -30)" />. They
      sit on opposite ends of a diameter — exactly as they should,
      because the x-face and the y-face are physically ninety degrees
      apart on the element. Check it: their midpoint is (50, 0), and
      each lies 42.4 from that centre. Same circle, drawn from two
      different starting faces.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Here is the aha. Rotate the physical element by an angle θ, and
      the corresponding point on the circle walks around the centre by
      <em>2θ</em>. The diameter stays rigid; it just spins. To read off
      the stress on any new orientation, find the angle 2θ around the
      circle and look at the coordinates of that point — those are your
      σ' and τ'. Doubling the angle is the price you pay for getting a
      circle instead of an ellipse, and it is a bargain.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Walk the diameter until shear vanishes — that is, until it lies
      flat on the σ-axis. The two endpoints there are the
      <em>principal stresses</em>: the orientation where shear is zero
      and the stress is purely normal, giving the largest and smallest
      normal stress any face through this point will ever see. For our
      numbers, <MathExpression latex="\sigma_1 = C + R \approx 92.4" />
      MPa and <MathExpression latex="\sigma_2 = C - R \approx 7.6" />
      MPa. The element is physically rotated by half of that 2θ — here
      about 22.5° — to reach the principal orientation.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Now walk the diameter another ninety degrees around the circle
      (so 45° on the element) and you arrive at the top and bottom of
      the circle. Those points have τ equal to the radius itself, so
      <MathExpression latex="\tau_{\max} = R \approx 42.4" /> MPa, on
      faces whose normal stress is the centre value, 50 MPa. Maximum
      shear and pure-normal stress live a quarter-turn apart on the
      circle, exactly 45° apart on the element — the same 45° you see
      on the slip lines of a ductile tensile specimen.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Load the <strong>Pure shear</strong> preset
      (<MathExpression latex="\sigma_x = \sigma_y = 0" />,
      <MathExpression latex="\tau_{xy} = 50" />). The centre collapses
      to the origin and the radius is 50, so the principal stresses are
      <MathExpression latex="+50" /> and <MathExpression latex="-50" />
      MPa — equal tension and compression on faces rotated 45° from the
      shear faces. That is why a chalk stick twisted in your hands
      fractures on a clean diagonal: pure torsion creates a tensile
      principal stress that pulls the brittle fibres apart along the
      spiral.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      So Mohr's circle is the compact picture of every stress
      orientation at one point. The very next demo, the
      <strong>3D stress state visualizer</strong>, generalises the
      same trick to three principal stresses and three circles instead
      of one. Then <strong>failure criteria</strong> takes those
      principal stresses and asks the next question — at what
      combination does the material actually yield? — and the featured
      problem <em>"Maximum shear in a thin-walled pressure vessel — via
      Mohr's circle"</em> walks an end-to-end design calculation along
      exactly that path.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Maximum shear in a thin-walled pressure vessel — via Mohr's circle"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 33, FA20."
>
  <p>
    A closed cylindrical tank with radius <MathExpression latex="r" />,
    wall thickness <MathExpression latex="t \ll r" />, and internal
    gauge pressure <MathExpression latex="p" /> sits under a biaxial
    stress state in the wall. Find the principal stresses and the
    maximum in-plane and absolute shear.
  </p>
  <p>
    Cutting the tank longitudinally and balancing pressure × projected
    area against wall tension gives the hoop stress; cutting it
    transversely gives the axial stress:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\sigma_{\text{hoop}} = \frac{p r}{t}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{axial}} = \frac{p r}{2 t}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{radial}} \approx 0"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The wall is already at principal orientation — no shear on the
    hoop/axial cut, so these <em>are</em> the in-plane principals.
    Mohr's circle for the wall's surface has centre and radius:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\sigma_{\text{avg}} = \tfrac{3 p r}{4 t}, \qquad \tau_{\max,\,\text{in-plane}} = \tfrac{\sigma_{\text{hoop}} - \sigma_{\text{axial}}}{2} = \tfrac{p r}{4 t}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    But the absolute maximum shear in 3D is the largest radius among
    the three Mohr circles drawn between every pair of principal
    stresses. Pair the hoop stress
    <MathExpression latex="\sigma_1 = pr/t" /> with the radial
    <MathExpression latex="\sigma_3 \approx 0" />:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\tau_{\max,\,\text{3D}} = \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_3}{2} = \frac{p r}{2 t}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The 3D maximum is <em>twice</em> the in-plane maximum — the failure
    plane is oriented at 45° to the radial direction, not on the wall's
    surface at all. This is the moment in the course when "always check
    the 3D circle" stopped being a rote rule and started being a habit:
    Tresca yield on a thin-walled vessel is set by the radial direction
    you forgot existed.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Euler buckling load for a pinned-pinned column"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 33, FA20."
>
  <p>
    A straight slender column of length <MathExpression latex="L" />,
    flexural rigidity <MathExpression latex="EI" />, pinned at both
    ends, under axial compressive load <MathExpression latex="P" />.
    Find the load at which a small lateral perturbation no longer
    decays — the critical buckling load.
  </p>
  <p>
    Imagine the column deflected by a tiny amount <MathExpression latex="y(x)" />.
    Moment equilibrium of the deflected shape gives the linear ODE:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="E I\, \frac{d^2 y}{d x^2} + P y = 0"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Boundary conditions <MathExpression latex="y(0) = y(L) = 0" />.
    Setting <MathExpression latex="k^2 = P/(EI)" />, the general
    solution is <MathExpression latex="y(x) = A \sin kx + B \cos kx" />.
    The <MathExpression latex="y(0)=0" /> condition kills
    <MathExpression latex="B" />; <MathExpression latex="y(L)=0" />
    then requires <MathExpression latex="\sin kL = 0" />, i.e.
    <MathExpression latex="kL = n\pi" /> for some integer
    <MathExpression latex="n \geq 1" />. The smallest non-trivial load
    is <MathExpression latex="n = 1" />:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="P_{\text{cr}} = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{L^2}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Two facts worth internalising. First, <MathExpression latex="P_{\text{cr}}" />
    depends on <MathExpression latex="I" />, not the cross-sectional
    area — geometry, not material quantity, controls buckling. Doubling
    a column's radius increases <MathExpression latex="I" /> by 16×.
    Second, the <MathExpression latex="1/L^2" /> scaling is brutal:
    doubling the length quarters the critical load. End conditions
    enter through an effective-length factor
    <MathExpression latex="L_{\text{eff}}" /> — fixed-free is
    <MathExpression latex="2L" />, fixed-fixed is <MathExpression latex="L/2" />.
    The Euler-buckling visualizer above plots these four end-condition
    curves side by side; the slenderness ratio
    <MathExpression latex="L/r" /> where Euler stops applying
    (material yields first) is where the dashed asymptote crosses.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Cantilever-beam tip deflection v(L) = -PL³/(3EI) by double integration"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 33, FA20."
>
  <p>
    A horizontal cantilever of length <MathExpression latex="L" /> and
    flexural rigidity <MathExpression latex="EI" /> is clamped at the
    wall and carries a downward point load
    <MathExpression latex="P" /> at the free tip. Show that the tip
    deflection is
    <MathExpression latex="v(L) = -PL^3 / (3EI)" />.
  </p>
  <p>
    The reaction at the wall is an upward force
    <MathExpression latex="P" /> plus a moment
    <MathExpression latex="PL" /> resisting tip rotation. Cut the beam
    at coordinate <MathExpression latex="x" /> measured from the wall;
    equilibrium of the segment to the right gives the internal moment
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="M(x) = -P(L - x)"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The Euler–Bernoulli beam equation
    <MathExpression latex="EI\, v''(x) = M(x)" /> integrates twice:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="EI\, v'(x) = -P L x + \tfrac{1}{2} P x^2 + C_1,\qquad EI\, v(x) = -\tfrac{1}{2} P L x^2 + \tfrac{1}{6} P x^3 + C_1 x + C_2"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Apply the clamped boundary conditions
    <MathExpression latex="v(0) = 0" /> and
    <MathExpression latex="v'(0) = 0" /> (no displacement, no rotation
    at the wall): both integration constants vanish. Evaluate the
    deflection at the tip:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="v(L) = \frac{1}{EI}\Bigl( -\tfrac{1}{2} P L^3 + \tfrac{1}{6} P L^3 \Bigr) = -\frac{P L^3}{3 E I}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The <MathExpression latex="L^3" /> dependence is the punchline.
    Double the cantilever length and the same tip load gives eight
    times the deflection — a 2× safety factor on length costs much
    more than a 2× safety factor on stiffness. Substitute
    <MathExpression latex="I = b h^3 / 12" /> for a rectangular
    section and the same cubic punishes thin beams that flex in the
    weak direction: rotate the cross-section 90° (load on the
    long edge instead of the short one) and the deflection drops by
    <MathExpression latex="(h/b)^2" />. The beam-deflection
    visualizer above plots these curves directly — sweep
    <MathExpression latex="L" />, <MathExpression latex="h" />,
    <MathExpression latex="b" /> and watch the cubic show up
    immediately.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>
