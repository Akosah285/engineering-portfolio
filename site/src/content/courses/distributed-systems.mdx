<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="d'Alembert solution to the 1D wave equation"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 23, WI20."
>
  <p>
    The 1D wave equation
    <MathExpression latex="u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}" /> on the infinite line
    with initial displacement <MathExpression latex="u(x,0) = \varphi(x)" />
    and initial velocity <MathExpression latex="u_t(x,0) = \psi(x)" />.
    Solve in closed form and read off what kinds of disturbances
    propagate at what speed.
  </p>
  <p>
    Change variables: <MathExpression latex="\xi = x - ct" />,
    <MathExpression latex="\eta = x + ct" />. The PDE becomes
    <MathExpression latex="u_{\xi\eta} = 0" />, which integrates twice
    to give <em>any</em> sum of a right-moving and left-moving wave:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="u(x,t) = F(x - ct) + G(x + ct)"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Match the two initial conditions:
    <MathExpression latex="F(x) + G(x) = \varphi(x)" /> and
    <MathExpression latex="-c F'(x) + c G'(x) = \psi(x)" />. Solving
    for <MathExpression latex="F" /> and <MathExpression latex="G" />
    yields the d'Alembert formula:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="u(x,t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[\varphi(x-ct) + \varphi(x+ct)\bigr] + \tfrac{1}{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct} \psi(s)\, ds"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The geometry is striking: the value of <MathExpression latex="u" />
    at <MathExpression latex="(x,t)" /> depends only on initial data
    inside the past light cone
    <MathExpression latex="[x-ct,\, x+ct]" /> — finite signal speed
    falls out of the math. An initial displacement splits into two
    half-amplitude copies travelling outward at speed
    <MathExpression latex="\pm c" />. An initial velocity profile
    contributes a smeared, integrated tail — which is why striking a
    drumhead (impulsive velocity) makes a longer-lived disturbance than
    plucking a string (instantaneous displacement). The Charge Field
    Plotter above tells the static side of the same story for
    electrostatics; the wave equation is where time enters and
    everything starts propagating.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Magnetic field on the axis of a circular current loop"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 23, WI20."
>
  <p>
    A circular loop of radius <MathExpression latex="R" /> carries
    steady current <MathExpression latex="I" />. Find the magnetic
    field <MathExpression latex="B(z)" /> on the axis, a distance
    <MathExpression latex="z" /> from the loop's centre.
  </p>
  <p>
    The Biot–Savart law for a current element
    <MathExpression latex="I\, d\boldsymbol\ell" /> at position
    <MathExpression latex="\mathbf r'" /> contributes
    <MathExpression latex="d\mathbf B = (\mu_0/4\pi)\, I\, d\boldsymbol\ell \times (\mathbf r - \mathbf r')/|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|^3" />
    at the field point. Each loop element is distance
    <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}" /> from the axis point,
    and the cross product gives a vector of magnitude
    <MathExpression latex="R\, d\ell" /> tilted off-axis by angle
    <MathExpression latex="\theta" /> with
    <MathExpression latex="\sin\theta = R/\sqrt{R^2+z^2}" />.
    Symmetry kills the perpendicular components when integrated
    around the loop, leaving only the axial projection:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="B_z(z) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\oint \frac{R}{(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}\, d\ell = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2 (R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Two limits worth checking. At the centre
    (<MathExpression latex="z=0" />)
    <MathExpression latex="B = \mu_0 I / (2R)" />, the standard
    single-loop result. For
    <MathExpression latex="z \gg R" />, expanding gives
    <MathExpression latex="B \approx \mu_0 I R^2 / (2 z^3)" /> — the
    far field of any planar current loop is a magnetic dipole with
    moment <MathExpression latex="m = I \pi R^2" />, and the
    <MathExpression latex="1/z^3" /> fall-off is the signature of a
    dipole.
  </p>
  <p>
    Stack two such loops separated by a distance equal to their
    radius (Helmholtz coils) and the cubic non-uniformity in the
    midplane cancels — the field is uniform to leading order over a
    surprisingly large central region. The Biot–Savart visualizer
    above shows this directly: drop two loops in coaxially, vary the
    separation, and watch the central field flatten near
    <MathExpression latex="d = R" />.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>
