## 1D wave equation (string)

Plucked-string wave equation with reflective boundary conditions.

<Explainer demoTitle="1D wave equation (string)">
  <Step>
    <p>
      Pluck a guitar string. The shape moves, oscillates, returns,
      oscillates again. What single equation captures every plucked string,
      every taut wire, every acoustic-pipe mode? The 1D wave equation does,
      and it falls out of nothing more exotic than Newton's second law
      applied to a tiny segment of string.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The equation itself is two lines:
      <MathExpression display latex="\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \, \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}" />
      where <em>u</em>(<em>x</em>,<em>t</em>) is the string's vertical
      displacement and <em>c</em> is the wave speed (set by tension and
      mass density). Acceleration in time equals curvature in space, scaled
      by <MathExpression latex="c^2" />.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The physical picture: a tiny segment of string is pulled by tension
      on both sides. If the string is straight there
      (<MathExpression latex="u_{xx} = 0" />), the side forces cancel and
      nothing happens. If the string is curved upward
      (<MathExpression latex="u_{xx} > 0" />), the segment gets yanked
      upward proportional to the curvature. That is the acceleration
      <MathExpression latex="u_{tt}" /> — pure Newton.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The boundary conditions tie this physics to the demo. A string
      clamped at both ends gives Dirichlet zero:
      <MathExpression latex="u(0,t) = u(L,t) = 0" /> for all time. Those
      fixed endpoints are what force discrete <em>standing-wave</em>
      solutions to exist at all — without them you would have free
      travelling waves and never a clean mode shape.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The closed-form standing waves are
      <MathExpression display latex="u(x,t) = \sin\!\left(\tfrac{m\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\!\left(\tfrac{c m \pi t}{L}\right)" />
      for integer mode number <em>m</em> = 1, 2, 3, ... The "Fundamental"
      preset is <em>m</em> = 1 (a single half-sine); the "Second harmonic"
      preset is <em>m</em> = 2 with a node at the midpoint.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Numerically the demo does not use the closed form — it integrates
      the PDE on a grid using the explicit leapfrog scheme:
      <MathExpression display latex="u^{n+1}_i = 2 u^n_i - u^{n-1}_i + r^2 (u^n_{i+1} - 2 u^n_i + u^n_{i-1})" />
      That parenthesised piece is the discrete second spatial derivative,
      and <MathExpression latex="r = c \, dt / dx" /> is the ratio of
      "physical wave speed" to "grid signal speed."
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Stability hinges on one inequality: the
      <em>Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy</em> (CFL) condition
      <MathExpression latex="r = c \, dt / dx \le 1" />. Violate it and the
      leapfrog scheme explodes in a few iterations, producing wild
      oscillations that have nothing to do with the physics. Satisfy it
      and the numerical solution tracks the analytical standing waves to
      well within plotting accuracy.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The intuition for CFL is physical: each timestep, information can
      hop at most one grid cell. If the true wave moves faster than one
      grid cell per timestep, the numerical scheme is missing data it
      should have used, and it diverges. So when you crank up <em>c</em>
      or shrink <em>dx</em>, you must shrink <em>dt</em> in lockstep.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Load the "High resolution (200 pts)" preset and look at <em>dt</em>:
      it drops to 0.002 (versus 0.005 in the fundamental preset) precisely
      because <em>dx</em> shrank. Now mentally raise <em>c</em> to 5 — the
      CFL bound forces <em>dt</em> below 0.001. This trade is why
      high-resolution wave simulations are so expensive.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The featured problem <em>"Standing-wave frequencies on a fixed-end
      string by separation of variables"</em> just below derives those
      mode shapes from scratch by assuming
      <MathExpression latex="u(x,t) = X(x) T(t)" />. For the
      travelling-wave (rather than standing-wave) perspective, the
      <em>"d'Alembert solution to the 1D wave equation"</em> featured
      problem decomposes any initial pulse into a left-mover plus a
      right-mover. To see what happens when waves on a 2D membrane meet
      each other, jump to the 2D wave interference demo immediately below.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="d'Alembert solution to the 1D wave equation"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 23, WI20."
>
  <p>
    The 1D wave equation
    <MathExpression latex="u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}" /> on the infinite line
    with initial displacement <MathExpression latex="u(x,0) = \varphi(x)" />
    and initial velocity <MathExpression latex="u_t(x,0) = \psi(x)" />.
    Solve in closed form and read off what kinds of disturbances
    propagate at what speed.
  </p>
  <p>
    Change variables: <MathExpression latex="\xi = x - ct" />,
    <MathExpression latex="\eta = x + ct" />. The PDE becomes
    <MathExpression latex="u_{\xi\eta} = 0" />, which integrates twice
    to give <em>any</em> sum of a right-moving and left-moving wave:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="u(x,t) = F(x - ct) + G(x + ct)"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Match the two initial conditions:
    <MathExpression latex="F(x) + G(x) = \varphi(x)" /> and
    <MathExpression latex="-c F'(x) + c G'(x) = \psi(x)" />. Solving
    for <MathExpression latex="F" /> and <MathExpression latex="G" />
    yields the d'Alembert formula:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="u(x,t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[\varphi(x-ct) + \varphi(x+ct)\bigr] + \tfrac{1}{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct} \psi(s)\, ds"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The geometry is striking: the value of <MathExpression latex="u" />
    at <MathExpression latex="(x,t)" /> depends only on initial data
    inside the past light cone
    <MathExpression latex="[x-ct,\, x+ct]" /> — finite signal speed
    falls out of the math. An initial displacement splits into two
    half-amplitude copies travelling outward at speed
    <MathExpression latex="\pm c" />. An initial velocity profile
    contributes a smeared, integrated tail — which is why striking a
    drumhead (impulsive velocity) makes a longer-lived disturbance than
    plucking a string (instantaneous displacement). The Charge Field
    Plotter above tells the static side of the same story for
    electrostatics; the wave equation is where time enters and
    everything starts propagating.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Magnetic field on the axis of a circular current loop"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 23, WI20."
>
  <p>
    A circular loop of radius <MathExpression latex="R" /> carries
    steady current <MathExpression latex="I" />. Find the magnetic
    field <MathExpression latex="B(z)" /> on the axis, a distance
    <MathExpression latex="z" /> from the loop's centre.
  </p>
  <p>
    The Biot–Savart law for a current element
    <MathExpression latex="I\, d\boldsymbol\ell" /> at position
    <MathExpression latex="\mathbf r'" /> contributes
    <MathExpression latex="d\mathbf B = (\mu_0/4\pi)\, I\, d\boldsymbol\ell \times (\mathbf r - \mathbf r')/|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|^3" />
    at the field point. Each loop element is distance
    <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}" /> from the axis point,
    and the cross product gives a vector of magnitude
    <MathExpression latex="R\, d\ell" /> tilted off-axis by angle
    <MathExpression latex="\theta" /> with
    <MathExpression latex="\sin\theta = R/\sqrt{R^2+z^2}" />.
    Symmetry kills the perpendicular components when integrated
    around the loop, leaving only the axial projection:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="B_z(z) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\oint \frac{R}{(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}\, d\ell = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2 (R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Two limits worth checking. At the centre
    (<MathExpression latex="z=0" />)
    <MathExpression latex="B = \mu_0 I / (2R)" />, the standard
    single-loop result. For
    <MathExpression latex="z \gg R" />, expanding gives
    <MathExpression latex="B \approx \mu_0 I R^2 / (2 z^3)" /> — the
    far field of any planar current loop is a magnetic dipole with
    moment <MathExpression latex="m = I \pi R^2" />, and the
    <MathExpression latex="1/z^3" /> fall-off is the signature of a
    dipole.
  </p>
  <p>
    Stack two such loops separated by a distance equal to their
    radius (Helmholtz coils) and the cubic non-uniformity in the
    midplane cancels — the field is uniform to leading order over a
    surprisingly large central region. The Biot–Savart visualizer
    above shows this directly: drop two loops in coaxially, vary the
    separation, and watch the central field flatten near
    <MathExpression latex="d = R" />.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Standing-wave frequencies on a fixed-end string by separation of variables"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 23, WI20."
>
  <p>
    A string of length <MathExpression latex="L" /> with both ends
    pinned obeys the 1D wave equation
    <MathExpression latex="u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}" /> with boundary
    conditions <MathExpression latex="u(0, t) = u(L, t) = 0" /> for
    all time. Show that the only frequencies that can stand on this
    string are
    <MathExpression latex="f_n = n c / (2 L)" /> for
    <MathExpression latex="n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots" />.
  </p>
  <p>
    Assume a separated solution
    <MathExpression latex="u(x, t) = X(x) T(t)" />. Substituting into
    the PDE and dividing by <MathExpression latex="X(x) T(t)" />
    gives
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\frac{T''(t)}{c^2\, T(t)} = \frac{X''(x)}{X(x)} = -\lambda"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The left side depends only on <MathExpression latex="t" />, the
    right only on <MathExpression latex="x" />, so both must equal a
    constant — call it <MathExpression latex="-\lambda" /> with the
    sign chosen so non-decaying oscillation is possible. The spatial
    equation <MathExpression latex="X'' + \lambda X = 0" /> with
    <MathExpression latex="X(0) = X(L) = 0" /> is a classic
    Sturm–Liouville problem whose only non-trivial solutions are
    <MathExpression latex="X_n(x) = \sin(n \pi x / L)" /> with
    eigenvalues <MathExpression latex="\lambda_n = (n \pi / L)^2" />.
  </p>
  <p>
    The temporal equation
    <MathExpression latex="T'' + c^2 \lambda_n T = 0" /> oscillates
    at angular frequency
    <MathExpression latex="\omega_n = c \sqrt{\lambda_n} = n \pi c / L" />,
    i.e. linear frequency
    <MathExpression latex="f_n = n c / (2 L)" />. The fundamental is
    <MathExpression latex="f_1 = c / (2 L)" />; every higher mode is
    an integer multiple of it — the harmonic series that gives
    plucked-string instruments their consonant character.
  </p>
  <p>
    Lab 3 used this exact relation in reverse. With
    <MathExpression latex="L" /> measured directly and
    <MathExpression latex="f_1" /> read off the spectrum analyser,
    <MathExpression latex="c = 2 L f_1" /> recovered the wave speed
    on the string to within a few percent of the value predicted from
    string density and tension. The wave-equation visualizer above
    lets you set fixed ends and pluck initial conditions, then watch
    the d'Alembert traveling waves bounce, recombine, and self-
    organise into the predicted standing-wave envelope.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Method of images — a point charge above a grounded conducting plane"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 23, WI20."
>
  <p>
    A point charge <MathExpression latex="+q" /> sits a distance
    <MathExpression latex="d" /> above an infinite grounded
    conducting plane at <MathExpression latex="z = 0" />. Find the
    electrostatic potential in the upper half-space, the induced
    surface charge on the conductor, and the force pulling the real
    charge towards the plane. Solving Laplace's equation directly with
    the boundary condition
    <MathExpression latex="\Phi(x, y, 0) = 0" /> is painful. Solving it
    by the <em>method of images</em> is two lines.
  </p>
  <p>
    Replace the conductor with a fictitious image charge
    <MathExpression latex="-q" /> at <MathExpression latex="(0, 0, -d)" />,
    in the empty half-space below. The superposition of real and image
    potentials,
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\Phi(x, y, z) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z-d)^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z+d)^2}}\right]"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    is identically zero on <MathExpression latex="z = 0" />, satisfies
    Laplace's equation everywhere in <MathExpression latex="z > 0" />
    (the only singularity is the real charge), and matches the
    physical configuration at infinity. By the uniqueness theorem for
    Laplace's equation with Dirichlet boundary data, <em>this is the
    answer</em>. The induced surface charge follows from
    <MathExpression latex="\sigma = -\varepsilon_0 \, \partial \Phi / \partial z|_{z=0} = -q d / [2\pi (r^2 + d^2)^{3/2}]" />,
    which integrates to a total of <MathExpression latex="-q" /> on
    the plane.
  </p>
  <p>
    The force on the real charge is the force exerted by the image
    charge alone (the conductor is just a mathematical fiction):
    <MathExpression latex="F = -q^2 / (16 \pi \varepsilon_0 d^2)" />,
    attractive, falling off as <MathExpression latex="1/d^2" /> as if
    the charge were attracted by a single mirror partner. The wave-
    and field-visualisers on this page run the same uniqueness logic
    in motion — replace a boundary with the right virtual source, and
    the messy PDE collapses into a free-space superposition.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>
