<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Karnaugh-map minimisation of a 4-variable Boolean function"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth Digital Electronics, SP20."
>
  <p>
    Minimise the sum-of-products form of
    <MathExpression latex="F(A,B,C,D)" /> where the function is
    1 for the canonical "majority-of-three-inputs" pattern across
    <MathExpression latex="\{A,B,C\}" /> with <MathExpression latex="D" />
    acting as an enable: <MathExpression latex="F = D \cdot \mathrm{Maj}(A,B,C)" />.
  </p>
  <p>
    The truth table has eight 1-cells where exactly two or three of
    <MathExpression latex="A,B,C" /> are high <em>and</em>
    <MathExpression latex="D=1" />. Arrange on a 4-variable K-map (rows
    in <MathExpression latex="AB" /> Gray order, columns in
    <MathExpression latex="CD" /> Gray order). The 1-cells form three
    overlapping pairs of adjacent squares — each pair eliminates one
    variable:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="F = ABD + ACD + BCD"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Three prime implicants, each a product of three literals, total
    nine literals. Checking essentialness: cell
    <MathExpression latex="ABCD" /> is covered by all three implicants
    so contributes no constraint; cells
    <MathExpression latex="ABC'D" />,
    <MathExpression latex="AB'CD" />,
    <MathExpression latex="A'BCD" />
    are each covered by exactly one of the three implicants — so all
    three are essential.
  </p>
  <p>
    The cost of <em>not</em> minimising: the canonical sum-of-minterms
    form has 8 terms × 4 literals = 32 literals, ≈ 32 transistors per
    NAND-implementation of each AND gate. K-map minimisation gives 9
    literals — a roughly 3.5× reduction in gate count for an identical
    function. The K-map minimiser demo above runs this same algorithm
    interactively; the gain extends to dozens-of-input designs where
    hand minimisation breaks down and Quine–McCluskey or Espresso
    takes over.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Sequence detector for '1011' — Moore vs. Mealy state count"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth Digital Electronics, SP20."
>
  <p>
    Design a finite-state machine that asserts its output on every
    occurrence of the bit pattern <code>1011</code> in a serial input
    stream (overlapping matches allowed). Compare the Moore and Mealy
    implementations on state count and timing.
  </p>
  <p>
    The natural Moore machine tracks "how much of <code>1011</code>
    have we matched so far?" with five states:
    <MathExpression latex="S_0" /> (no match),
    <MathExpression latex="S_1" /> (matched <code>1</code>),
    <MathExpression latex="S_2" /> (matched <code>10</code>),
    <MathExpression latex="S_3" /> (matched <code>101</code>),
    <MathExpression latex="S_4" /> (matched <code>1011</code>, output
    high). Transitions handle overlap: from
    <MathExpression latex="S_4" /> on input <code>1</code> jump back
    to <MathExpression latex="S_1" /> (the trailing <code>1</code>
    becomes the start of a new match); on input <code>0</code> drop
    to <MathExpression latex="S_2" /> via reusing the
    <code>1</code> at position 3. State register width:
    <MathExpression latex="\lceil \log_2 5 \rceil = 3" /> bits.
  </p>
  <p>
    The Mealy version collapses the output-only state
    <MathExpression latex="S_4" /> into a labelled transition. Four
    states (<MathExpression latex="S_0, S_1, S_2, S_3" />), with the
    output asserted on the transition
    <MathExpression latex="S_3 \xrightarrow{1/1} S_1" />. State
    register: 2 bits. The output now depends combinationally on the
    current input, which means it appears one clock cycle earlier than
    in the Moore machine — but it inherits any glitches on the input.
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\text{States: Moore} = 5, \quad \text{Mealy} = 4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta\,\text{FFs} = 1"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The textbook trade-off in one example. Moore costs an extra flip
    flop but guarantees glitch-free, register-aligned outputs; Mealy
    saves the flop and gives faster response at the price of
    combinational outputs that need careful timing closure. The FSM
    simulator above lets you toggle between the two encodings on this
    exact problem; on a real datapath you pick Moore when the output
    drives another sequential block, Mealy when latency dominates.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>
