<Explainer demoTitle="Epicycle drawing">
  <Step>
    <p>
      You've seen this trick before, even if you didn't know its name. A
      Spirograph is a small gear rolling inside a bigger one, with a pen
      sitting on the small gear that traces an intricate flower-shaped
      curve. Astronomers from Ptolemy onwards modelled the planets as
      circles rolling on circles — "epicycles" — to fit the wandering
      paths they saw in the night sky. The viral video of Homer Simpson's
      outline drawn by a chain of rotating arms is the same recipe. This
      demo runs that recipe backwards: hand it any closed shape and it
      works out which arms you need.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The claim is bold: any closed planar curve — heart, square,
      signature, cartoon outline — can be traced by stacking rotating
      arms. One arm spins at one revolution per cycle. The next is
      mounted on the tip of the first and spins at two revolutions, the
      next at three, and so on (plus the same set spinning backwards).
      Pick the right length and starting angle for each arm and their
      combined tip draws your shape exactly. The slider labelled
      <em>Num terms</em> is how many arms you let the chain use.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Treat the canvas as the complex plane, so a point on the curve is
      <MathExpression latex="z = x + iy" />. The recipe is the Fourier
      series of a closed curve:
    </p>
    <MathExpression
      latex="z(t) = \sum_{k} c_k\, e^{i\, 2\pi k t}"
      display={true}
    />
    <p>
      Each term <MathExpression latex="c_k e^{i\, 2\pi k t}" /> is exactly
      one rotating arm: its length is <MathExpression latex="|c_k|" />,
      its starting angle is <MathExpression latex="\arg c_k" />, and the
      integer <em>k</em> says how many turns it makes per loop. Adding
      the terms is tip-to-tail vector addition — the rotating chain you
      watch on the canvas.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Start with the simplest preset: load <strong>Circle</strong> and
      pull <em>Num terms</em> down to 1. The shape is already a pure
      rotation, so the analysis puts everything in
      <MathExpression latex="c_1" /> and nothing anywhere else. One arm
      of length 1 spinning once per cycle traces the circle exactly.
      Push <em>Num terms</em> back up and the extra arms have amplitude
      zero, so they sit invisibly at the joint — no approximation, no
      leftover wiggle.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Now load <strong>Heart</strong>. The visualiser doesn't see your
      curve as a formula — it samples 128 evenly-spaced points along it,
      one per parameter
      <MathExpression latex="t \in \{0/128, 1/128, \ldots, 127/128\}" />.
      Those 128 complex samples go into a discrete Fourier transform,
      which returns 128 complex coefficients <MathExpression latex="c_k" />.
      Each one is a length and a starting angle for an arm at integer
      frequency <em>k</em>. Crank the <em>Sample points</em> slider up
      and you get more frequencies to choose from.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Out of 128 coefficients, the heart's energy lives in just a
      handful of low frequencies. The demo sorts them by
      <MathExpression latex="|c_k|" /> — biggest arm first — and the
      largest dozen already give a recognisable heart. With 1 arm the
      tip traces a circle. With 4 it's a lumpy oval. By 12 it's clearly
      a heart, and by 24 the wobble is gone. Each new arm is a smaller
      correction sitting on top of the previous fit.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Why does the transform recover the right arm lengths? Because the
      complex exponentials are orthogonal: averaging
      <MathExpression latex="e^{i\, 2\pi j t}\, \overline{e^{i\, 2\pi k t}}" />
      over one full cycle gives zero unless <MathExpression latex="j = k" />,
      and 1 when they match. So multiplying your samples by
      <MathExpression latex="e^{-i\, 2\pi k t}" /> and averaging picks out
      only the <MathExpression latex="c_k" /> component — every other arm
      cancels itself out in the integral. That is the entire DFT,
      summarised in one orthogonality identity.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      You'll notice the chain uses both positive and negative
      frequencies. A positive-<em>k</em> arm spins anti-clockwise; a
      negative-<em>k</em> arm spins clockwise. You need both because a
      real-valued curve forces a symmetry on the coefficients,
      <MathExpression latex="c_{-k} = \overline{c_k}" />, and the
      imaginary parts of the two paired arms cancel only when the
      clockwise twin is present. Drop the negative-frequency arms and
      the heart tilts off into a spiral.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Load the <strong>Square wave</strong> preset and slide
      <em>Num terms</em> from 1 up to 32. The straight edges fall into
      place with just a few arms, but each corner is a discontinuity in
      the derivative that needs many high-frequency arms to sharpen.
      Watch the residual wobble near each corner refuse to flatten
      cleanly — that overshoot is the same Gibbs phenomenon analysed in
      the featured problem
      <em>"Gibbs phenomenon at a square-wave discontinuity"</em> below,
      drawn on a closed loop instead of a flat line.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      This is the same idea as the <strong>Fourier series builder</strong>
      below, rotated into the complex plane: real sines and cosines
      become complex exponentials, and "sum of harmonics" becomes "chain
      of rotating arms." The two demos are paired — one stacks harmonics
      vertically against a waveform, the other stacks them tip-to-tail
      around the origin. The convolution animation and the FT-of-signals
      demo then extend the same machinery from periodic loops to
      non-periodic signals, which is where the featured problems pick
      the story up.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

## Featured problems

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Gibbs phenomenon at a square-wave discontinuity"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 92, FA20."
>
  <p>
    Sum the first <MathExpression latex="N" /> nonzero harmonics of a
    square wave and stare at the jump at <MathExpression latex="x=0" />.
    The partial sum overshoots the true height of the discontinuity by
    a fixed fraction — about 8.95% — and that overshoot does not shrink
    as <MathExpression latex="N \to \infty" />. Why?
  </p>
  <p>
    Take a unit-height square wave with Fourier series:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="s_N(x) = \frac{4}{\pi} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \frac{\sin\bigl((2k+1)x\bigr)}{2k+1}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Differentiating the partial sum gives a Dirichlet-style kernel that
    peaks just to one side of the jump. The peak location moves toward
    <MathExpression latex="x=0" /> as
    <MathExpression latex="N" /> grows (it sits at roughly
    <MathExpression latex="\pi/(2N)" />), but the <em>height</em> of the
    first overshoot is the integral of <MathExpression latex="\sin t / t" />
    from 0 to <MathExpression latex="\pi" />:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\lim_{N\to\infty} s_N\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2N}\right) = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi \frac{\sin t}{t}\, dt \approx 1.0895"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The reason the overshoot doesn't decay: as <MathExpression latex="N" />
    increases, the wiggle gets narrower (its support scales like
    <MathExpression latex="1/N" />), but its peak value is governed by
    a fixed integral. So in <MathExpression latex="L^2" /> the partial
    sums converge to the square wave just fine — the energy of the
    overshoot vanishes — yet they fail to converge pointwise at a
    discontinuity. The Fourier-series demo above shows this directly:
    push the harmonic count up and the ripples narrow without
    flattening. This is the moment in the course where I stopped
    thinking "more harmonics = better approximation" naïvely and started
    distinguishing the modes of convergence.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Fourier transform of e^(-|t|) by contour integration"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 92, FA20."
>
  <p>
    Compute the Fourier transform of the two-sided exponential
    <MathExpression latex="f(t) = e^{-|t|}" /> two ways: directly by
    splitting the integral at zero, and via a contour integral that
    invokes the residue theorem on the inverse transform.
  </p>
  <p>
    Direct evaluation is one line of bookkeeping:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\hat f(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-|t|} e^{-i\omega t}\, dt = \int_0^\infty e^{-(1+i\omega)t}\, dt + \int_0^\infty e^{-(1-i\omega)t}\, dt = \frac{2}{1+\omega^2}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The result is the Lorentzian. The interesting machinery shows up
    when you run the inversion. The inverse transform is
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{2 e^{i\omega t}}{1+\omega^2}\, d\omega"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Treat <MathExpression latex="\omega" /> as a complex variable. The
    integrand has simple poles at <MathExpression latex="\omega = \pm i" />.
    For <MathExpression latex="t > 0" /> close the contour in the upper
    half-plane (where <MathExpression latex="e^{i\omega t}" /> decays);
    only the pole at <MathExpression latex="\omega = i" /> sits inside,
    and the residue is
    <MathExpression latex="\mathrm{Res}_{\omega=i} = e^{-t} / (2i)" />.
    The residue theorem gives
    <MathExpression latex="f(t) = (1/2\pi)(2\pi i)(e^{-t}/(2i)\cdot 2) = e^{-t}" />.
    For <MathExpression latex="t<0" /> close in the lower half-plane,
    pick up the <MathExpression latex="\omega = -i" /> pole, and recover
    <MathExpression latex="e^{t}" />. Together: <MathExpression latex="e^{-|t|}" />,
    as required. This is the example that finally made me see contour
    integration as a <em>computational</em> tool rather than a curiosity —
    the residue-helper demo above is the exact bookkeeping in a sandbox.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Sinc orthogonality — why band-limited signals reconstruct exactly"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 92, FA20."
>
  <p>
    Sample a signal at Shannon's rate <MathExpression latex="f_s = 2B" />
    and the Whittaker–Shannon interpolation formula
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="f(t) = \sum_n f(n/f_s)\, \mathrm{sinc}\bigl( f_s(t - n/f_s) \bigr)"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    is exact for any signal whose Fourier transform vanishes outside
    <MathExpression latex="[-B, B]" />. The proof reduces to a single
    orthogonality identity:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\langle \mathrm{sinc}(2B(t - n/2B)),\ \mathrm{sinc}(2B(t - m/2B)) \rangle = \frac{1}{2B}\, \delta_{nm}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Apply Parseval. The Fourier transform of
    <MathExpression latex="\mathrm{sinc}(2B(t - n/2B))" /> is
    <MathExpression latex="(1/2B)\, \mathrm{rect}(v/2B)\, e^{-i 2\pi v n / 2B}" />.
    The inner product of two such sincs in the time domain equals the
    inner product of their Fourier transforms — and the product of
    those transforms is the rect window (constant on
    <MathExpression latex="[-B, B]" />, zero elsewhere) times a
    complex exponential at frequency
    <MathExpression latex="(n - m)/2B" />. Integrating over the rect
    interval gives <MathExpression latex="(1/2B)\, \mathrm{sinc}(n - m)" />,
    which is <MathExpression latex="1/2B" /> when
    <MathExpression latex="n = m" /> and zero at every other integer.
  </p>
  <p>
    So the shifted sincs are an orthogonal basis for the space of
    band-limited <MathExpression latex="L^2" /> signals — and the
    expansion coefficients are literally the sample values
    <MathExpression latex="f(n/f_s)" />. That's the entire content of
    the sampling theorem in one Parseval move. The FT-of-signals
    visualizer above shows the sinc &harr; rect duality that makes
    the integral collapse; the convolution-animation demo is the same
    identity rotated 90°.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Time-bandwidth product — why the Gaussian saturates Δt · Δω ≥ 1/2"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 32, FA20."
>
  <p>
    Define the time- and frequency-domain spreads of a normalised
    signal <MathExpression latex="f(t)" /> with transform
    <MathExpression latex="\hat{f}(\omega)" /> by the second moments
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="(\Delta t)^2 = \int t^2 |f(t)|^2 \, dt,\qquad (\Delta \omega)^2 = \int \omega^2 |\hat{f}(\omega)|^2 \, \frac{d\omega}{2\pi}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    (assuming the signal is centred at the origin in both domains and
    normalised by Plancherel). The Fourier uncertainty principle
    asserts
    <MathExpression latex="\Delta t \cdot \Delta \omega \geq \tfrac{1}{2}" />
    with equality only for Gaussian pulses. The proof is the
    Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to
    <MathExpression latex="\langle t f, f' \rangle" /> plus one
    integration by parts.
  </p>
  <p>
    Write <MathExpression latex="i \omega \hat{f} = \widehat{f'}" />,
    so by Plancherel
    <MathExpression latex="\Delta \omega^2 = \int |f'(t)|^2 \, dt" />.
    Cauchy–Schwarz gives
    <MathExpression latex="\left| \int t f(t) \overline{f'(t)} \, dt \right|^2 \leq \Delta t^2 \cdot \Delta \omega^2" />.
    The left-hand side equals
    <MathExpression latex="\left| \tfrac{1}{2} \int t \, (|f|^2)' \, dt \right|^2 = \tfrac{1}{4} \left| \int |f|^2 \, dt \right|^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}" />
    after one integration by parts on the assumption
    <MathExpression latex="|f|^2 \to 0" /> at infinity. Combining:
    <MathExpression latex="\Delta t^2 \cdot \Delta \omega^2 \geq 1/4" />.
  </p>
  <p>
    Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz requires
    <MathExpression latex="f'(t) = -k t f(t)" /> for some
    <MathExpression latex="k > 0" />, whose only square-integrable
    solution is the Gaussian
    <MathExpression latex="f(t) \propto e^{-kt^2/2}" />. That single
    inequality re-skins as the Heisenberg uncertainty principle in
    quantum mechanics (where it becomes
    <MathExpression latex="\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \hbar / 2" />),
    as the diffraction limit in optics, and as the time–frequency
    trade-off that forces a short pulse to be wideband (and vice
    versa). The spectrogram demo on this page is the same inequality
    drawn on a screen — squeeze the analysis window to sharpen onset
    timing and you'll watch frequency resolution blur in lockstep.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>
