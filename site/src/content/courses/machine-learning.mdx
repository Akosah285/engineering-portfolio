<Explainer demoTitle="Gradient descent">
  <Step>
    <p>
      Imagine you're hiking down into a valley wrapped in thick fog. You
      can't see the bottom, you can't see ten metres ahead — but you can
      feel the slope of the ground under your boots. So you do the only
      sensible thing: turn to face whichever way is steepest downhill,
      take a step, and repeat. Eventually, if the valley is well-behaved,
      you reach the lowest point. That is gradient descent. The whole
      algorithm. Every other detail is figuring out how big the step
      should be, and what to do when the terrain misbehaves.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      To turn that picture into maths, you need three things. The terrain
      is the loss function <MathExpression latex="L(x, y)" /> — every
      point in the plane has a height, and we want the lowest. The slope
      under your feet is the gradient <MathExpression latex="\nabla L" /> —
      it points in the direction of steepest <em>increase</em>, so the
      negative gradient points downhill. And the learning rate
      <MathExpression latex="\eta" /> sets how big a step you take. The
      update is one line:
    </p>
    <MathExpression
      latex="x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\, \nabla L(x_t)"
      display={true}
    />
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Let's do one step by hand on the simplest surface. The
      <strong> Bowl</strong> is <MathExpression latex="L(x, y) = x^2 + y^2" />,
      shaped like the inside of a salad bowl with its minimum at the
      origin. Its gradient is <MathExpression latex="\nabla L = (2x, 2y)" />.
      Start at (2.5, 1.8); the gradient there is (5.0, 3.6). With
      <MathExpression latex="\eta = 0.05" /> the update subtracts
      0.05·(5.0, 3.6) = (0.25, 0.18). Your new position is (2.25, 1.62).
      The loss dropped from 9.49 to 7.69. One step closer to the bottom.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Repeat that update and the trajectory walks straight into (0, 0).
      On a bowl the negative gradient always points roughly toward the
      centre, so every step gets you closer. Load the
      <strong> Bowl (vanilla)</strong> preset and watch the green dot do
      exactly this — it slows down as it approaches the minimum because
      the gradient itself shrinks. Near the bottom the slope is gentle,
      so the steps shrink in proportion and the iterates settle in.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The single most important knob is <MathExpression latex="\eta" />.
      Drag the <strong>Learning rate</strong> slider on the Bowl preset.
      At <MathExpression latex="\eta = 0.001" /> the dot crawls — each
      step is so timid it barely moves. At
      <MathExpression latex="\eta = 0.05" /> it lands neatly. Past about
      <MathExpression latex="\eta = 0.5" /> the step is so long it
      overshoots the bottom and lands on the <em>opposite</em> wall of
      the bowl, higher than it started. Crank it further and every step
      is longer than the last; the iterates fling off to infinity.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Vanilla gradient descent treats each step independently — every
      iteration restarts from rest. <strong>Momentum</strong> keeps a
      running velocity <MathExpression latex="v" /> that the gradient
      nudges, with coefficient <MathExpression latex="\beta" /> recycling
      the previous velocity:
    </p>
    <MathExpression
      latex="v_t = \beta\, v_{t-1} - \eta\, \nabla L(x_{t-1}), \quad x_t = x_{t-1} + v_t"
      display={true}
    />
    <p>
      A consistent downhill direction builds speed; transient zig-zags
      cancel. Compare <strong>Bowl (vanilla)</strong>
      (<MathExpression latex="\beta = 0" />) with
      <strong> Bowl (momentum)</strong>
      (<MathExpression latex="\beta = 0.9" />) — momentum reaches the
      minimum in roughly a quarter of the steps.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Where momentum really pays off: load the
      <strong> Rosenbrock valley</strong> preset. The surface is a
      narrow, curving canyon with the minimum at (1, 1). The gradient
      mostly points <em>across</em> the canyon walls — there's barely
      any slope along the long axis. Vanilla GD bounces wall-to-wall
      and crawls toward the minimum at a snail's pace. Momentum lets
      the long-axis component build up while the cross-wall
      oscillations cancel themselves out, so the dot threads the
      canyon at speed.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The other failure modes are on display in the remaining presets.
      <strong> Saddle escape</strong> drops you near a saddle point where
      the gradient is almost zero — vanilla GD stalls there indefinitely;
      momentum eventually drifts off the ridge.
      <strong> Vanishing plateau</strong> is the deep-learning nightmare:
      far from the minimum the gradient shrinks to almost nothing, so
      each step is tiny no matter what <MathExpression latex="\eta" /> you
      pick. Cranking the learning rate or adding momentum is what
      unsticks you.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Load <strong>Rosenbrock valley</strong>, then drag momentum
      <MathExpression latex="\beta" /> down to 0 while keeping
      <MathExpression latex="\eta" /> at 0.001. Watch the dot pin itself
      ricocheting between the canyon walls and barely move along the
      valley floor. Now push <MathExpression latex="\beta" /> back up to
      0.95 with the same <MathExpression latex="\eta" /> and the dot
      suddenly threads the canyon — the gradient hasn't changed, you've
      just stopped throwing away the history of where you were going.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      This same update is the engine underneath almost every model on
      this page. The <strong>polynomial regression</strong> demo below
      fits its coefficients by gradient descent on a least-squares loss,
      and the K-means image compression demo runs an analogous
      alternating minimisation that descends a clustering objective at
      each pass. The Featured Solution
      <em> Gradient descent convergence for an L²-regularised quadratic </em>
      makes the η story from step 5 precise — it proves the sharp bound
      on how large the learning rate can be before the iterates bounce
      instead of converging, and generalises everything you just watched
      to any convex quadratic.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

## Reflection

Machine Learning & Statistical Data Analysis was where the foundations clicked
for me — the math behind the algorithms more than the algorithms themselves.
Linear algebra from a prior term meant the gradient updates and matrix
manipulations weren't the obstacle; the conceptual hurdle was K-Nearest
Neighbors, of all things — wrapping my head around why "just compare to the
nearest points" was a principled approach took longer than expected.

The projects ranged across spam detection, price prediction, and cancer
classification, and each one drove home the same lesson: there isn't a single
right model — there's iteration toward the right balance for the problem
you're solving. The piece that's stayed with me longest, though, isn't an
algorithm. It's the habit of thinking about bias — in the data, in the model,
in the framing of the question. That generalizes far beyond ML.

The course was during COVID, and if I took it again I'd push deeper on the
final project and chase the extra-credit material harder.

## K-means image compression — clustering as quantisation

<Explainer demoTitle="K-means image compression">
  <Step>
    <p>
      Open a JPEG of a sunset on your phone and the file claims it uses
      thousands of distinct colours — sweeping oranges, pinks, dusty
      purples, near-blacks in the corners. Your eye can't actually
      distinguish most of them. Now imagine you're only allowed to keep
      sixteen colours, and you have to snap every pixel to whichever of
      those sixteen it most resembles. Pick the sixteen well and the
      image still looks like a sunset; pick them badly and it looks like
      paint-by-numbers. K-means is the algorithm that picks them well —
      automatically, from the image itself.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Strip the image away and start with six dots scattered on a page.
      We want two groups; that's it. Drop two markers anywhere — call
      them seeds. Walk to each dot in turn, ask which seed it is closer
      to, and colour the dot to match. You've made a first guess at the
      clustering.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Now slide each seed to the average position of the dots wearing
      its colour — its new centre of gravity. Some dots are suddenly
      closer to the other seed than the one they're wearing, so
      reassign them. Recompute the two centres again. Repeat. After a
      handful of rounds nothing changes — the seeds and the colourings
      stop moving and the clustering has settled.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      That assign-then-update loop <em>is</em> the whole algorithm.
      There's no derivative, no learning rate, no gradient. Each
      iteration does two cheap things — relabel every point, then
      recompute every centre — and the structure emerges from
      repetition alone. It's the simplest non-trivial unsupervised
      method in the toolkit.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      What is the loop actually optimising? Call
      <MathExpression latex="x_i" /> a data point and
      <MathExpression latex="\mu_{c(i)}" /> the centre of the cluster
      it currently belongs to. Then the quantity we drive down is the
      within-cluster sum of squares
      <MathExpression latex="J = \sum_i \|x_i - \mu_{c(i)}\|^2" />,
      sometimes called the <em>inertia</em> — total squared distance
      from every point to its own centre.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Here is the aha: the loop is coordinate descent on
      <MathExpression latex="J" />. The assignment step holds the
      centres fixed and picks the labels that minimise
      <MathExpression latex="J" /> — each point picks its nearest
      centre, by definition the smallest contribution. The update step
      holds the labels fixed and picks the centres that minimise
      <MathExpression latex="J" /> — the mean is provably the point
      that minimises sum of squared distances to a set. Neither step
      can ever increase <MathExpression latex="J" />, so the algorithm
      always converges.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Converges, yes — but not always to the best clustering. The loop
      is greedy and gets stuck at whichever local minimum of
      <MathExpression latex="J" /> the initial seeds happen to fall
      into. Drop two seeds into the same dense blob and one cluster
      will starve while the other does all the work.
      <strong>k-means++</strong> fixes most of this by spreading the
      seeds out — each new seed is sampled with probability
      proportional to its squared distance from the closest seed
      already chosen.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Now snap back to the image. Each pixel is a point in 3D RGB
      space — red, green, blue all between 0 and 255 — so a photo with
      thousands of unique colours is just thousands of points in a
      cube. K-means with <MathExpression latex="K = 16" /> hunts for
      the sixteen points in that cube whose neighbourhoods cover the
      colours best, then every pixel is recoloured to its nearest
      cluster centre. That is the entire compression.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Load the <strong>Sunset palette</strong> sample and slide K down
      to 2. The image collapses to two colours — a warm and a cool —
      and the soft gradient becomes a hard band. Slide K up to 16 and
      the bands fade back into a smooth sky. Then flip to
      <strong>Four-color checkerboard</strong> with K = 4: K-means
      recovers the exact four originals and the reconstruction is
      pixel-perfect.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      This assign-then-update structure is more familiar than it looks.
      The <strong>gradient descent</strong> demo at the top of the page
      is coordinate descent in disguise once you fix the step size, and
      the <strong>polynomial regression</strong> demo next door is yet
      another loop that optimises a sum of squared distances — just
      with a parameter vector instead of a label per point. The same
      picture keeps recurring across the course: define an objective,
      alternate the variables you minimise over, and watch it converge.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Bias–variance decomposition for a polynomial fit"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 89, SP20."
>
  <p>
    Suppose we draw <MathExpression latex="n" /> noisy samples from a smooth
    target <MathExpression latex="y = f(x) + \varepsilon" /> with
    <MathExpression latex="\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0" /> and
    <MathExpression latex="\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2" />, fit a
    polynomial <MathExpression latex="\hat f" /> of degree
    <MathExpression latex="d" /> by least squares, and ask: what determines
    the test error at a fixed query point <MathExpression latex="x" />?
  </p>
  <p>
    Adding and subtracting <MathExpression latex="\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)]" />
    inside the squared term and taking expectations over both the training
    set and the noise gives the canonical decomposition:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\mathbb{E}\left[ \left( y - \hat f(x) \right)^2 \right] = \underbrace{\left( \mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)] - f(x) \right)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\hat f(x)\bigr)}_{\text{variance}} + \sigma^2"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The lesson is that the three pieces pull in different directions. As
    <MathExpression latex="d" /> grows, bias shrinks (the model can chase
    finer wiggles in <MathExpression latex="f" />) but variance grows (the
    model also chases the noise). The irreducible <MathExpression latex="\sigma^2" />
    sits there regardless of how clever the model is. In the polynomial-regression
    demo above, sliding the degree past about 8 on a 20-point sample is
    where I watch variance start to dominate — the curve starts threading
    individual noisy points instead of the underlying trend. Turning on
    ridge with a small <MathExpression latex="\lambda" /> doesn't change the
    decomposition; it shifts the operating point by trading a little extra
    bias for a much larger reduction in variance.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Reflection"
  title="Why K-Nearest Neighbors is principled, not just intuitive"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 89, SP20."
>
  <p>
    KNN felt like a hand-wave the first time I saw it — "just compare
    against the nearest points" — and of every algorithm in the course it
    took the longest to feel like a principled estimator rather than a
    heuristic. Gradient descent on a regression loss I can write down and
    differentiate; KNN doesn't train, it just memorises. The discomfort
    was: where is the math?
  </p>
  <p>
    The reframe that fixed it: KNN is a non-parametric estimate of the
    posterior <MathExpression latex="P(y = c \mid x)" /> with a bandwidth
    set implicitly by <MathExpression latex="k" />:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\hat P(y = c \mid x) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i \in N_k(x)} \mathbb{1}\{y_i = c\}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Once it's a posterior estimate, the rest of the picture falls into
    place. The decision rule <MathExpression latex="\hat y = \arg\max_c \hat P(y=c\mid x)" />
    is just the plug-in Bayes classifier. Cover and Hart's classical
    result that 1-NN error is at most twice the Bayes error in the
    asymptotic limit stops being mystical — it's a statement about how
    well a 1-sample posterior estimate approximates the truth.
    Cross-validating <MathExpression latex="k" /> is selecting a
    bandwidth, exactly the same problem as picking a kernel width.
  </p>
  <p>
    The lesson generalised beyond KNN: every "lazy" or memory-based
    learner I've met since — kernel ridge regression, Gaussian processes,
    even retrieval-augmented language models — sits at some point on the
    same axis of "how much structure do I impose on the posterior, and
    how much do I read off the data".
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Gradient descent convergence for an L²-regularised quadratic"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 89, SP20."
>
  <p>
    Take a convex quadratic with an L² penalty and ask the question
    every implementer of gradient descent eventually has to answer: how
    large can the learning rate <MathExpression latex="\eta" /> be before
    the iterates stop converging?
  </p>
  <p>
    With objective
    <MathExpression latex="J(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{2} \theta^\top H \theta + b^\top \theta + \tfrac{\lambda}{2} \|\theta\|^2" />
    (symmetric positive semi-definite <MathExpression latex="H" />), the
    batch gradient step is:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \bigl( (H + \lambda I)\theta_t + b \bigr)"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Let <MathExpression latex="\theta^\star = -(H + \lambda I)^{-1} b" />
    be the unique minimiser (the regulariser makes the inverse exist
    even when <MathExpression latex="H" /> is singular). Subtracting the
    fixed-point equation from the update gives a linear recurrence for
    the error <MathExpression latex="e_t = \theta_t - \theta^\star" />:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="e_{t+1} = \bigl( I - \eta (H + \lambda I) \bigr)\, e_t"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Diagonalise <MathExpression latex="H + \lambda I = Q \Lambda Q^\top" />.
    In the eigenbasis each coordinate of the error decays by the scalar
    <MathExpression latex="1 - \eta \mu_i" /> per step, where
    <MathExpression latex="\mu_i" /> ranges over the eigenvalues of
    <MathExpression latex="H + \lambda I" />. Monotone descent in every
    coordinate requires
    <MathExpression latex="|1 - \eta \mu_i| < 1" /> for all
    <MathExpression latex="i" />, which gives the sharp bound:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="0 < \eta < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}(H) + \lambda}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Two things I find satisfying about this. First, the convergence
    rate is set by the eigenvalue <em>ratio</em>
    <MathExpression latex="\kappa = \lambda_{\max} / \lambda_{\min}" /> —
    so ridge regularisation literally improves convergence by shrinking
    <MathExpression latex="\kappa" /> (push <MathExpression latex="\lambda" />
    up and the denominator's smallest eigenvalue rises). Second, the
    gradient descent visualizer at the top of the page is exactly this
    story in two dimensions: pick an elongated loss surface, crank the
    learning rate, and you see the eigenvalue-1 direction overshoot
    while the eigenvalue-0.1 direction still creeps along the long axis.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>
