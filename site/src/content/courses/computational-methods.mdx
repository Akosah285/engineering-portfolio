## Bisection method

Bracket-and-halve root finding with a live convergence trace.

<Explainer demoTitle="Bisection method">
  <Step>
    <p>
      Suppose I ask you to find <em>x</em> such that
      <em>x</em>² = 2. Your calculator returns 1.41421356... in a
      millisecond, but how does it actually do that? You can't solve it on
      paper. Bisection is the simplest answer — the slowest, most reliable
      algorithm in the toolkit, and the one every "fancy" method falls back
      to when it gets confused.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The whole trick is one observation about continuous functions. If
      <em>f</em>(<em>a</em>) is negative and <em>f</em>(<em>b</em>) is
      positive, then the graph has to cross zero <em>somewhere</em> between
      <em>a</em> and <em>b</em>. We don't know where yet, but we know the
      root is trapped in the interval. Bisection's job is to shrink that
      trap until it is small enough to call the answer.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Look at <MathExpression latex="f(x) = x^2 - 2" /> on the interval
      <MathExpression latex="[0, 2]" />. We have
      <MathExpression latex="f(0) = -2" /> (negative) and
      <MathExpression latex="f(2) = 2" /> (positive), so a root is trapped
      inside. Cut the interval in half. The midpoint is 1, and
      <MathExpression latex="f(1) = -1" /> is still negative. So the sign
      change must happen between 1 and 2 — we just threw half the work
      away.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Do it again on <MathExpression latex="[1, 2]" />: midpoint 1.5,
      <MathExpression latex="f(1.5) = 0.25" /> (positive), so the root is
      now in <MathExpression latex="[1, 1.5]" />. Again on
      <MathExpression latex="[1, 1.5]" />: midpoint 1.25,
      <MathExpression latex="f(1.25) = -0.4375" />, so root is in
      <MathExpression latex="[1.25, 1.5]" />. Four steps in we are at
      <MathExpression latex="[1.375, 1.4375]" /> — already two decimal
      digits of <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{2}" />.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Every step exactly halves the width of the bracket. After 10 steps
      the trap is 1/1024 of its original size. After 20 steps it is one in
      a million. After 50 it is smaller than a double-precision float can
      even represent. That is the entire convergence story — the bracket
      width is <MathExpression latex="(b_0 - a_0) / 2^n" /> after
      <em>n</em> steps, and you stop when it is below your tolerance.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      To get one more decimal digit of precision, you need just over three
      more bisection steps (because 2³ = 8 and 2⁴ = 16, sandwich 10
      between them). So 21 steps gets you 6 decimal digits, 30 gets you 9,
      50 gets you ~15. The demo's default tolerance is
      <MathExpression latex="10^{-6}" />, which is why it halts around
      iteration 21.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The catch is that bisection is <em>slow</em>. Every step buys you
      the same constant factor of improvement — one bit. Newton's method,
      which we will meet next, can buy you doubling-digits per step when
      it works. But bisection's superpower is that it always works.
      It never diverges, never oscillates, never needs a derivative.
      It cannot fail as long as its preconditions hold.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Those preconditions are everything: <em>f</em> must be continuous,
      and the starting interval must have a sign change. Skip either and
      the IVT argument breaks. A jump discontinuity inside
      <MathExpression latex="[a, b]" /> can let
      <MathExpression latex="f(a) \cdot f(b) < 0" /> be true with no
      actual root in between — the graph "crosses" the axis by
      teleporting over it.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Load the <strong>Slow shrink</strong> preset
      (<MathExpression latex="f(x) = (x-1)^7" />). The function sits very
      close to zero on both sides of the root, so just checking
      <MathExpression latex="|f(m)| < \varepsilon" /> would let the method
      "converge" while the bracket is still huge. The demo also checks
      that the bracket width has shrunk — that "or" keeps the
      high-multiplicity case honest.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      So that is bisection: the bracketing baseline that every faster
      root-finder is compared to. The very next demo, <strong>Newton's
      method</strong>, throws away bracketing in exchange for using the
      derivative to take much bigger steps — and the featured problem
      <em>"Quadratic convergence of Newton's method near a simple root"</em>
      counts exactly how much faster Newton is when it works. Read them
      back-to-back: bisection gives you one bit per step always; Newton
      gives you doubling-digits per step until it doesn't.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

## Newton's method

Tangent-line iteration with quadratic-convergence visualisation.

<Explainer demoTitle="Newton's method">
  <Step>
    <p>
      Bisection earns you one bit of precision per step. If you also know
      the slope of the function, you can do much better — converging in
      about five steps instead of twenty for the same accuracy. That is
      Newton's method, and the leap from "one bit per step" to "doubling
      digits per step" is one of the most useful speedups in all of
      numerical computing.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The geometric idea: at your current guess, draw the tangent line to
      the graph. The tangent crosses the x-axis at one specific point —
      that crossing becomes your <em>next</em> guess. Up close, any smooth
      function looks almost like a straight line, so the tangent's
      prediction of where the graph hits zero is almost exactly right. Then
      you repeat from the new spot.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      A worked example with <MathExpression latex="f(x) = x^2 - 2" /> and
      starting guess <MathExpression latex="x_0 = 1.5" />: we have
      <MathExpression latex="f(1.5) = 0.25" /> and slope
      <MathExpression latex="f'(1.5) = 3" />. The tangent line crosses
      zero at <MathExpression latex="1.5 - 0.25/3 \approx 1.4167" />.
      That's our next guess. Check it:
      <MathExpression latex="f(1.4167) \approx 0.0069" /> — already two
      decimal digits closer to <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{2}" /> from
      one step.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The pattern that produces is the famous Newton formula:
      <MathExpression display latex="x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}" />
      Read it as: "from where you are now, step backward by (current
      value) divided by (current slope)." If the value is already small,
      the step is small. If the slope is steep, the step is small. If the
      slope is gentle, the step is big.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      What makes Newton fast is the famous doubling rule: each step
      <em>squares</em> the error rather than halving it. If you start with
      one digit of accuracy, the next step gives two, then four, then
      eight, then sixteen. With the square-root preset, five iterations
      take you from 1.5 to <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{2}" /> accurate
      to a dozen digits — past anything a single-precision float could
      represent.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Why does this work? Near a smooth root, the function genuinely is
      almost a straight line, and the tangent <em>is</em> that line. So
      the new error is roughly the previous error squared — small numbers
      squared get tiny fast. This is what mathematicians call
      <em>quadratic convergence</em>, and it is the gold standard for
      iterative algorithms.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The catch is that Newton is fragile. If your starting guess is far
      from the root, or the slope is nearly horizontal, or the function
      has a kink, Newton can wander off in surprising directions. It can
      cycle. It can shoot to infinity. It can land on a different root
      than the one you wanted. Bisection, in contrast, just slowly does
      its job.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The "Bad seed (cycle)" preset shows the worst case. With
      <MathExpression latex="f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2" /> seeded at
      <MathExpression latex="x_0 = 0" />: f(0) = 2, slope = -2, tangent
      crosses zero at <MathExpression latex="0 - 2/(-2) = 1" />. Now from
      <em>x</em> = 1: f(1) = 1, slope = 1, tangent crosses at
      <MathExpression latex="1 - 1/1 = 0" />. We're back where we
      started, and the iteration ping-pongs between 0 and 1 forever.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Run the "Quadratic convergence" preset (sine, seed 3.0). Pause
      after each step and read off <MathExpression latex="|f(x_n)|" /> in
      the iterate table. You should see
      <MathExpression latex="10^{-2} \to 10^{-4} \to 10^{-8} \to 10^{-16}" />.
      That is the digit-doubling, made visible: each step squares the
      previous error.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      So the trade is concrete: bisection gives you one bit per step
      always; Newton gives you doubling-digits per step until it doesn't.
      The featured problem <em>"Quadratic convergence of Newton's method
      near a simple root"</em> below derives the error-squaring rule from
      a Taylor expansion. For the slow-but-bulletproof counterpart that
      needs no derivative, see the bisection demo just above. Production
      root finders like Brent's method actually cheat and combine both —
      Newton when it is racing, bisection as a safety net when it isn't.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

## Monte Carlo integration

Compare convergence rates of Monte Carlo integration against the trapezoidal
rule on the same integrand.

<Explainer demoTitle="Monte Carlo integration">
  <Step>
    <p>
      Imagine you want to know the area of an oddly-shaped pond in a
      rectangular field. You can't measure it directly, but you can
      throw a thousand pebbles at random across the field and count how
      many splash into the water. The fraction that splash, multiplied
      by the field's area, is a surprisingly good estimate of the
      pond's area. Monte Carlo integration is exactly that idea, dressed
      up for any function you can evaluate. No calculus required — just
      averages and a good random number generator.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Suppose you want to compute the integral of <em>f</em>(<em>x</em>)
      from <em>a</em> to <em>b</em>. Instead of slicing the interval
      into neat strips like the trapezoidal or Simpson's rule, you pick
      <em>N</em> points at random inside <MathExpression latex="[a, b]" />
      and evaluate <em>f</em> at each one. The average f-value tells
      you roughly how tall the function is on average, and multiplying
      by the interval width <MathExpression latex="(b - a)" /> gives you
      the area underneath. That is the whole algorithm. The cleverness
      is in believing it actually converges.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Let's estimate <MathExpression latex="\int_0^1 x^2\, dx" /> by
      hand. The exact answer is <MathExpression latex="1/3 \approx 0.3333" />.
      Pick four random x-values in <MathExpression latex="[0, 1]" /> —
      say 0.17, 0.42, 0.68, 0.91 — and square them: 0.029, 0.176, 0.462,
      0.828. The average is 0.374, and the interval width is 1, so our
      estimate is 0.374. With only four darts we are already in the
      right neighbourhood, off by about 12%. Bump the count to 1000 and
      the error typically drops below 2%.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Writing the recipe down:
    </p>
    <MathExpression
      latex="\int_a^b f(x)\, dx \approx \frac{b - a}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i)"
      display={true}
    />
    <p>
      where the points <MathExpression latex="x_i" /> are drawn
      uniformly from <MathExpression latex="[a, b]" />. That's it — no
      derivatives, no smoothness assumptions, no fancy weights. The
      same formula generalises to two, three, or twenty dimensions:
      replace <MathExpression latex="(b - a)" /> with the volume of the
      bounding box and draw points uniformly inside it. The integrator
      in this demo is roughly thirty lines of code.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Why does averaging random samples work at all? The Law of Large
      Numbers is the engine: as <em>N</em> grows, the sample average
      converges to the true average value of <em>f</em> on the
      interval. Each new dart you throw nudges the running mean a
      little closer to the right answer. More darts, more accurate
      average — that is the whole intuition. The same theorem
      underpins every opinion poll, every clinical trial, and every
      casino's pricing model.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The catch is how slowly it converges. The error shrinks like
      <MathExpression latex="1/\sqrt{N}" />, not like
      <MathExpression latex="1/N" />. To gain one decimal digit of
      accuracy you must multiply <em>N</em> by 100. Going from 1000
      samples to 100,000 only buys you one extra digit. The trapezoidal
      rule, by contrast, gives roughly two extra digits per
      ten-times-finer mesh in 1D. So for a smooth one-dimensional
      integrand, Monte Carlo is genuinely worse than the deterministic
      rules.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      So why bother? Because deterministic rules suffer the curse of
      dimensionality. To halve the trapezoidal-rule error in 20
      dimensions you need roughly a million times more grid points.
      Monte Carlo does not care — its
      <MathExpression latex="1/\sqrt{N}" /> rate is independent of
      dimension. By around four or five dimensions Monte Carlo starts
      winning, and by twenty it is the only thing that fits in memory.
      Financial risk modelling, particle physics, and graphics
      rendering all live in that high-dimensional regime.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      A nice bonus: the sample variance gives you a free error bar.
      The demo reports a 95% confidence half-width of about
      <MathExpression latex="1.96 \cdot s / \sqrt{N}" />, where
      <em>s</em> is the sample standard deviation of the f-values. So
      you do not just get an estimate — you get an honest "I'm 95% sure
      the true value lies within plus-or-minus this much" range. Plain
      quadrature has nothing comparable without a separate error
      analysis. With Monte Carlo the uncertainty is baked in.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Load the <strong>Runge</strong> preset
      (<MathExpression latex="\int_{-1}^{1} 1/(1 + 25 x^2)\, dx" />).
      Start at 100 samples and watch the estimate jump around — the
      confidence half-width is wide. Crank <em>N</em> up to 10,000 and
      the half-width should shrink by roughly
      <MathExpression latex="\sqrt{100} = 10" />. Then imagine
      sampling more densely where the integrand is tall and less where
      it is flat — clever sampling so each dart counts for more. That
      intuition is exactly what variance-reduction tricks like
      importance sampling do.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Read this one paired with the <strong>Numerical integration
      comparator</strong> above — they are the two halves of numerical
      integration. Quadrature spends a careful, deterministic budget
      of function evaluations and crushes smooth low-dimensional
      integrals. Monte Carlo spends a sloppy random budget and crushes
      high-dimensional ones. The same Strang chapter connects them:
      deterministic rules optimise the placement of <em>N</em> points;
      Monte Carlo gives up and lets randomness do the placing. Pick
      the right tool for your dimension and you will never look back.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Quadratic convergence of Newton's method near a simple root"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth MATH 56, SP20."
>
  <p>
    Newton's iteration
    <MathExpression latex="x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)" /> is famous
    for doubling the number of correct digits each step. Show this
    explicitly — and pin down what "near a simple root" means.
  </p>
  <p>
    Let <MathExpression latex="r" /> be a simple root
    (<MathExpression latex="f(r)=0" />, <MathExpression latex="f'(r) \neq 0" />)
    and write <MathExpression latex="e_n = x_n - r" /> for the error at
    step <MathExpression latex="n" />. Taylor-expand <MathExpression latex="f" />
    around <MathExpression latex="r" />:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="f(x_n) = f(r) + f'(r)\, e_n + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(\xi)\, e_n^2 = f'(r)\, e_n + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(\xi)\, e_n^2"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Substitute into the Newton update:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="e_{n+1} = e_n - \frac{f'(r)\, e_n + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(\xi)\, e_n^2}{f'(x_n)} = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2\, f'(x_n)}\, e_n^2"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    In the limit <MathExpression latex="x_n \to r" />,
    <MathExpression latex="f'(x_n) \to f'(r)" /> and the prefactor
    converges to <MathExpression latex="C = f''(r) / (2 f'(r))" />. So
    asymptotically <MathExpression latex="|e_{n+1}| \approx C\, e_n^2" />.
    Errors square per step — if <MathExpression latex="e_n = 10^{-3}" />,
    then <MathExpression latex="e_{n+1} \approx C \cdot 10^{-6}" />.
  </p>
  <p>
    The two caveats. First, "near": you need
    <MathExpression latex="|C \cdot e_0| < 1" /> for the recursion to
    contract — far from the root Newton can overshoot or diverge. The
    bisection visualizer above is the always-converges-but-linearly
    backstop you reach for when you don't have a good starting guess.
    Second, "simple": at a multiple root <MathExpression latex="f'(r)=0" />
    so the analysis breaks; Newton degrades to linear convergence
    unless you reweight (modified Newton with multiplicity
    <MathExpression latex="m" />:
    <MathExpression latex="x_{n+1} = x_n - m\, f/f'" />).
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Why RK4 is fourth-order — counting matched Taylor terms"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth MATH 56, SP20."
>
  <p>
    The classical Runge–Kutta integrator advances
    <MathExpression latex="y' = f(t,y)" /> by:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="y_{n+1} = y_n + \tfrac{h}{6}\bigl( k_1 + 2 k_2 + 2 k_3 + k_4 \bigr)"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    with slopes evaluated at the start, two midpoints, and the
    endpoint. Why the
    <MathExpression latex="1{:}2{:}2{:}1" /> weighting? And what does
    "fourth order" actually buy us?
  </p>
  <p>
    The exact solution Taylor-expanded around <MathExpression latex="t_n" />
    gives
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="y(t_n + h) = y_n + h y'_n + \tfrac{h^2}{2} y''_n + \tfrac{h^3}{6} y'''_n + \tfrac{h^4}{24} y^{(4)}_n + O(h^5)"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    where each <MathExpression latex="y^{(k)}" /> can be rewritten in
    terms of <MathExpression latex="f" /> and its partial derivatives
    using the chain rule (e.g.
    <MathExpression latex="y'' = f_t + f f_y" />). Now expand each
    slope <MathExpression latex="k_i" /> the same way; the weights
    <MathExpression latex="1, 2, 2, 1" /> divided by 6 are precisely
    the unique choice that matches every Taylor coefficient up through
    <MathExpression latex="h^4" />. The leftover error sits in the
    <MathExpression latex="h^5" /> term, so the local truncation error
    is <MathExpression latex="O(h^5)" /> and the global error over a
    fixed interval is <MathExpression latex="O(h^4)" /> — halving the
    step size cuts the error by a factor of 16.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the trade-off RK4 hits: four function evaluations per step
    for an order-of-magnitude advantage over forward Euler at any
    practical step size. Drop in the Lorenz system at the top of the
    page (chaotic, sensitive to step size) and RK4 is what keeps the
    trajectory recognisable while Euler at the same <MathExpression latex="h" />
    blows up in a few hundred steps. The cost-vs-accuracy sweet spot
    is the reason it's still everyone's default for non-stiff problems.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Conjugate Gradient solves an n×n SPD system in at most n steps"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth MATH 56, SP20."
>
  <p>
    Steepest Descent on an ill-conditioned SPD quadratic
    <MathExpression latex="\phi(x) = \tfrac12 x^T A x - b^T x" />
    zig-zags down the long axis of the contour ellipses and converges
    geometrically with rate <MathExpression latex="(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)" />,
    where <MathExpression latex="\kappa" /> is the condition number
    of <MathExpression latex="A" />. Conjugate Gradient does something
    startling: on an <MathExpression latex="n \times n" /> SPD system,
    it produces the exact solution in at most
    <MathExpression latex="n" /> iterations, regardless of conditioning.
  </p>
  <p>
    The mechanism is a strictly enlarging Krylov subspace. After
    <MathExpression latex="k" /> iterations, the CG iterate
    <MathExpression latex="x_k" /> is the unique minimiser of
    <MathExpression latex="\phi" /> (equivalently, of the
    <MathExpression latex="A" />-energy norm
    <MathExpression latex="\|x - x^\star\|_A" />) over the affine
    space <MathExpression latex="x_0 + \mathcal{K}_k(A, r_0)" /> with
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\mathcal{K}_k(A, r_0) = \mathrm{span}\bigl\{ r_0,\ A r_0,\ A^2 r_0,\ \ldots,\ A^{k-1} r_0 \bigr\}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    where <MathExpression latex="r_0 = b - A x_0" /> is the initial
    residual. Each CG step appends a fresh power of
    <MathExpression latex="A" /> to the subspace. After
    <MathExpression latex="n" /> steps the Krylov subspace fills
    <MathExpression latex="\mathbb{R}^n" /> (or terminates earlier if
    <MathExpression latex="r_0" /> happens to live in a smaller
    <MathExpression latex="A" />-invariant subspace), so the
    constrained minimiser equals the unconstrained minimiser equals
    <MathExpression latex="x^\star" />.
  </p>
  <p>
    The HW3 problem on a 3×3 SPD matrix bore this out exactly: CG
    converged in 3 iterations, Gauss–Seidel took about 6, and
    Steepest Descent ground through 16 before reaching comparable
    error. Practical CG on a 10,000×10,000 sparse system never
    actually runs <MathExpression latex="n" /> iterations — it
    terminates much earlier on a residual tolerance, exploiting
    clustered eigenvalues — but the
    <MathExpression latex="\le n" /> guarantee is what removes the
    conditioning anxiety that plagues most iterative solvers. The
    bisection visualizer up the page is the slow-but-sure cousin; CG
    is what you reach for when you have structure to exploit.
