<Explainer demoTitle="Solar tracker">
  <Step>
    <p>
      A sunflower follows the sun across the sky all day. Now picture
      a solar panel doing the same — not because it looks pretty, but
      because a panel pointed straight at the sun gathers roughly 40%
      more energy than one bolted flat to a roof. The question is how
      to make a £5 microcontroller do this without GPS, without an
      astronomical clock, and without knowing what time it is. The
      answer is wonderfully cheap: bolt two tiny light sensors to the
      panel, one on each side, and let them argue.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The sensors are LDRs — light-dependent resistors. Each one
      returns a number that grows when more light hits it. Mount one
      on the east edge of the panel and one on the west edge, and the
      brighter sensor tells you which way the sun is. If east reads
      higher, the sun is east of straight-ahead, so nudge the panel
      east. If west reads higher, nudge west. Repeat forever. That is
      the entire algorithm, and it does not care what hour it is or
      what latitude you are at.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Pick the <strong>Morning east</strong> preset. The east LDR
      reads 900, the west reads 200. Compute the difference:
      <MathExpression latex="\Delta = E - W = 900 - 200 = 700" />.
      Positive and large, so step east. The servo rotates by a small
      fixed amount — the demo uses 2° per step. After one step the
      panel sits 2° closer to the sun, both sensor readings shift
      slightly, and the next iteration recomputes from there.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Run this loop many times and the panel walks toward the sun in
      tiny steps. Early on the difference is huge, the algorithm
      steps every iteration, and the panel sweeps quickly. As the
      panel aligns, the east and west readings converge — the
      difference shrinks toward zero. Eventually the sensors disagree
      so little that stepping further would overshoot. That is when
      the controller should stop, not because it ran out of power,
      but because there is nothing useful left to do.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      That "stop when it is close enough" rule is the deadband.
      Concretely: <MathExpression latex="|\Delta| \le d \;\Rightarrow\; \text{hold}" />.
      With deadband <MathExpression latex="d = 5" />, the
      <strong>Noon balanced</strong> preset (E=600, W=600,
      <MathExpression latex="\Delta = 0" />) holds — obvious. But the
      <strong>Slightly east</strong> preset (E=505, W=500,
      <MathExpression latex="\Delta = 5" />) also holds, because 5 is
      right at the threshold. Without a deadband the panel would
      jitter east-west all day chasing one-unit sensor noise, draining
      the battery and grinding the servo for no measurable energy
      gain.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The full decision rule is just three branches:
    </p>
    <MathExpression
      latex="\text{dir} = \begin{cases} \text{hold} & |\Delta| \le d \\ \text{east} & \Delta > d \\ \text{west} & \Delta < -d \end{cases}"
      display={true}
    />
    <p>
      Two sensor reads, a subtraction, two comparisons, and a servo
      write. The whole microcontroller program is maybe twenty lines
      of C. No PID loop, no Kalman filter, no astronomical model — and
      yet a real garden-lamp tracker uses exactly this.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Two more knobs keep it well-behaved in the real world. The step
      size — 2° per iteration in the demo — sets how aggressively the
      panel chases the sun; smaller steps are smoother but slower,
      bigger steps overshoot and oscillate around the deadband. The
      angle is also clamped to <MathExpression latex="\pm 90°" /> so
      the panel cannot try to point at the ground when sunset confuses
      the sensors. The demo flags clamped steps in the angle history
      so you can see where the rule wanted to go versus where physics
      let it.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      What makes this work is that the two sensors <em>are</em> a
      gradient estimate. The difference <MathExpression latex="E - W" />
      is a finite-difference approximation to the spatial derivative
      of brightness across the panel, and stepping in its direction is
      the cheapest possible gradient ascent. You are optimising
      without ever writing down a cost function. In control-theory
      language it is a bang-bang controller with a dead zone — the
      same family as a thermostat that clicks the boiler on at 18°C
      and off at 20°C.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Load the <strong>Slightly east</strong> preset (E=505, W=500).
      With the default deadband of 5 the controller holds and the
      panel rests. Now drag the deadband slider down to 0. The same
      sensor readings now trigger an "east" decision every iteration,
      and the angle history starts crawling. That is the difference
      between a battery that lasts a year and one that dies in a
      month — decided by a single threshold value.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      So that is the tracker: two LDRs, a subtraction, a deadband, and
      a servo. The same bang-bang-with-deadband pattern recurs across
      the course — the <strong>traffic light</strong> FSM holds each
      phase until a timer or sensor fires, and the
      <strong>interrupt polling</strong> demo is the same "react when
      the signal crosses a threshold" idea pushed down into the CPU.
      Paired with the <strong>pwm</strong> visualiser, which is what
      actually drives the servo into motion, this demo is the front
      end of the embedded toolkit: turning a noisy analogue world into
      one of three discrete decisions.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

<Explainer demoTitle="Pulse-width modulation">
  <Step>
    <p>
      You want to dim an LED, but your microcontroller pin can only
      output two voltages — fully on at 5 V or fully off at 0 V. There
      is no "2.5 V" setting in the silicon, no analogue dial, no
      in-between. So how do you make the LED look half as bright? The
      answer is one of the most useful tricks in embedded systems, and
      it shows up every time you set a servo angle, control a fan, or
      fade an indicator.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The trick is to cheat with time. Instead of trying to hold the
      pin at 2.5 V, flick it between 5 V and 0 V very quickly, spending
      half of every tick at 5 V and half at 0 V. Your eye cannot follow
      individual flickers above roughly 100 per second, so it sees the
      time-average instead. The LED looks as if it were sitting at
      half-brightness — even though the actual voltage is always either
      fully on or fully off.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Load the <strong>Motor</strong> preset: 5 V high, 0 V low,
      switching 1000 times a second, on for half of each cycle. The
      average voltage the LED feels is just
      <MathExpression latex="5\,\text{V} \times 0.5 = 2.5\,\text{V}" />.
      Drop the on-fraction to 0.25 and the average becomes
      <MathExpression latex="5\,\text{V} \times 0.25 = 1.25\,\text{V}" /> —
      a dimmer LED, with no analogue circuitry anywhere. That
      on-fraction is the <strong>duty</strong>: the fraction of each
      cycle the pin is ON.
    </p>
    <MathExpression
      latex="V_\text{avg} = V_\text{low} + D \cdot (V_\text{high} - V_\text{low})"
      display={true}
    />
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      The whole thing only works because the load has <em>inertia</em>.
      An LED's brightness, a motor's shaft speed, a fan's airflow —
      none of them can change as fast as the pin can flip. They smooth
      out the rapid pulses into a steady average, the same way your ear
      hears a tone instead of individual speaker-cone wiggles. Switch
      faster than the load can react, and physics does the averaging
      for you.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      How many on/off pulses per second do you need? It depends on the
      load. An LED needs ~100 Hz or above so the eye stops seeing
      flicker. A small DC motor likes a few kHz, fast enough that the
      motor's coil-inductance averages the current. A servo wants
      exactly 50 Hz — its electronics decode the on-time itself, not
      the average voltage. Go too slow and the load follows each pulse;
      go too fast and the switching transistor wastes energy in every
      transition.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      For a brushed DC motor, torque is proportional to current, and
      current follows the average voltage across the coils. So 50% duty
      at 12 V gives roughly the same torque as a constant 6 V supply —
      but the H-bridge driver only ever sees ON or OFF, which is what a
      switching transistor is actually good at. That is why every cheap
      cordless drill, RC car, and laptop fan controls speed by PWM
      rather than by an analogue dimmer.
    </p>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      Resistive heaters break the average-voltage rule. A heater
      dissipates power as <MathExpression latex="V^2/R" />, which is
      non-linear in V, so you have to use the
      <strong>RMS</strong> (root-mean-square) voltage instead. For the
      <strong>Heater</strong> preset (12 V, 20% duty) the average is
      only 2.4 V, but the RMS works out to about 5.4 V — and it is the
      RMS that sets the heat output. Same waveform, two different
      "effective" voltages, picked by what the load cares about.
    </p>
    <MathExpression
      latex="V_\text{rms} = \sqrt{D \cdot V_\text{high}^2 + (1 - D) \cdot V_\text{low}^2}"
      display={true}
    />
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>Try it yourself.</p>
    <TryThis>
      Load the <strong>Servo</strong> preset (50 Hz, 10% duty). Now
      drag the duty up to 20%. The average voltage doubles from 0.5 V
      to 1.0 V — but a real servo would swing its arm from one limit
      to the other. Servos do not care about the average; their
      internal electronics decode the on-time directly (1 ms pulse =
      full left, 2 ms = full right). Same PWM waveform, completely
      different interpretation depending on what is wired to the pin.
    </TryThis>
  </Step>
  <Step>
    <p>
      So that is PWM: a fake analogue output built from fast digital
      pulses, with the load's own inertia doing the smoothing. The
      pattern recurs across the rest of the course — the
      <strong>solar tracker</strong>'s servo is driven by exactly the
      50 Hz pulse train above, the <strong>rpm</strong> demo measures
      the output of a PWM-driven motor by counting encoder edges, and
      the <strong>interrupt vs polling</strong> demo is the same idea
      in reverse: reacting to a pulse train instead of generating one.
      Paired with the <strong>adc sampling</strong> visualiser, which
      turns analogue voltages back into digital numbers, PWM closes the
      loop between digital pin and physical world.
    </p>
  </Step>
</Explainer>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Gamma-corrected PWM for perceived LED brightness"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 85, WI21."
>
  <p>
    An LED is dimmed by pulse-width modulation on a microcontroller
    GPIO at duty cycle <MathExpression latex="d \in [0,1]" />. A linear
    sweep of <MathExpression latex="d" /> from 0 to 1 looks visually
    nothing like a linear sweep of perceived brightness — most of the
    apparent change happens in the bottom 20% of duty. Pick the
    8-bit PWM compare value that produces a perceptually-linear
    brightness ramp.
  </p>
  <p>
    Human brightness perception follows a power law (CIE Lightness, or
    sRGB's gamma curve, both approximate). A serviceable model:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="L_{\text{perceived}} = d_{\text{linear}}^{1/\gamma}, \qquad \gamma \approx 2.2"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    To make perceived brightness linear in the user-facing 8-bit
    parameter <MathExpression latex="b \in \{0, \ldots, 255\}" />, set
    <MathExpression latex="L = b/255" /> and invert:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\mathrm{compare}(b) = \mathrm{round}\!\left( 255 \cdot \left(\tfrac{b}{255}\right)^{\!\gamma} \right)"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    For <MathExpression latex="b = 128" /> (halfway on the user
    slider) and <MathExpression latex="\gamma = 2.2" />, the compare
    value is <MathExpression latex="255 \cdot 0.5^{2.2} \approx 55" />
    — i.e. about 22% PWM duty, not 50%. That's exactly the gap
    between "physical halfway" and "perceptual halfway".
  </p>
  <p>
    Implementation note for an MCU: precompute the 256-entry lookup
    table in flash at build time. A runtime
    <MathExpression latex="\mathrm{pow}" /> on an 8-bit AVR costs
    hundreds of cycles per LED update; a table lookup is one load.
    For multi-channel RGB the same table applies per channel — gamma
    is the colour-space step you'd otherwise be doing in
    floating-point sRGB conversion. The PWM visualizer above shows
    the duty-vs-time waveform; the perceptual mapping is what
    determines what <em>value</em> you write into that compare
    register.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="Nyquist + ISR budget for a 1 kHz audio ADC on a 16 MHz MCU"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 85, WI21."
>
  <p>
    A 16 MHz microcontroller is sampling audio through its on-chip
    ADC. The conversion is timer-triggered; the ADC-complete
    interrupt service routine reads the result, runs a 16-tap FIR
    low-pass, and writes the output to a circular DMA buffer. Pick a
    sampling rate that respects Nyquist for a 1 kHz signal of
    interest and verify the ISR fits in the per-sample budget.
  </p>
  <p>
    Nyquist requires
    <MathExpression latex="f_s > 2 f_{\max}" />, so for a 1 kHz
    bandwidth the sample rate must exceed 2 kHz. With analogue
    anti-aliasing rolling off above the passband, a practical choice
    is <MathExpression latex="f_s = 8\,\text{kHz}" /> — a 4× margin
    that gives the digital filter room to do its own anti-aliasing
    without aliasing the upper transition band.
  </p>
  <p>
    Per-sample budget:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="T_s = \frac{1}{f_s} = 125\,\mu\text{s} = 16 \times 10^6 \cdot 125 \times 10^{-6} = 2000\ \text{CPU cycles}"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The 16-tap FIR at 8-bit input and 16-bit accumulator costs roughly
    16 multiply-accumulates × 4 cycles = 64 cycles, plus context
    save/restore on the AVR at ~30 cycles, plus the DMA write at ~10
    cycles. Round to 150 cycles per ISR.
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="\text{ISR occupancy} = \frac{150}{2000} = 7.5\%"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    A 7.5% interrupt load leaves 92.5% of the CPU for the rest of the
    application — comfortable. Push <MathExpression latex="f_s" /> to
    44.1 kHz (CD-quality audio) and the budget shrinks to
    <MathExpression latex="\approx 363" /> cycles per sample, FIR
    occupancy hits 41%, and you're suddenly forced to either drop the
    tap count, move to a hardware multiply, or hand off the filter to
    a DSP block. This is the calculation I do first on every
    interrupt-driven signal-chain design before writing any code; the
    ADC-sampling visualizer above is the per-sample shape that the
    ISR is processing.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>

<FeaturedProblem
  kind="Featured Solution"
  title="TMP36 + 10-bit ADC — resolving 1 °C needs 2 LSBs of headroom"
  attribution="Adapted from Dartmouth ENGS 85, WI21."
>
  <p>
    A TMP36 outputs a linear voltage proportional to ambient
    temperature:
    <MathExpression latex="V_{\rm out}\ [\mathrm V] = 0.01\, T\ [^\circ\mathrm C] + 0.5" />.
    Feed this into a 10-bit ADC referenced to 5 V, and the chain
    from raw count to temperature is two arithmetic steps — but the
    quantisation tells you the resolution you can actually trust.
  </p>
  <p>
    ADC counts to voltage:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="V = \frac{\mathrm{count}}{2^{10} - 1} \cdot V_{\rm ref} = \frac{\mathrm{count}}{1023} \cdot 5\ \mathrm V"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    Voltage to temperature, inverting the TMP36 linearisation:
  </p>
  <MathExpression
    latex="T\ [^\circ\mathrm C] = (V - 0.5) / 0.01 = 100\, V - 50"
    display={true}
  />
  <p>
    The LSB is
    <MathExpression latex="5\,\mathrm V / 1023 \approx 4.89\,\mathrm{mV}" />,
    which the TMP36's 10 mV/°C scaling turns into a temperature
    quantum of
    <MathExpression latex="\approx 0.489\,^\circ\mathrm C" />.
    Resolving a full 1 °C therefore takes 2 LSBs of headroom — fine
    for ambient-temperature logging, marginal for a fever-screening
    thermometer. To halve the quantum, drop
    <MathExpression latex="V_{\rm ref}" /> from 5 V to 2.5 V (an
    internal precision reference will do): the same 10-bit count now
    spans 2.5 V instead of 5 V, doubling the resolution at the cost
    of clipping any TMP36 reading above
    <MathExpression latex="(2.5 - 0.5)/0.01 = 200\,^\circ\mathrm C" />
    (still safely above any ambient lab condition).
  </p>
  <p>
    The bigger source of error in practice was rarely the ADC — it
    was the TMP36's ±2 °C accuracy spec at 25 °C and the supply-rail
    noise coupling into <MathExpression latex="V_{\rm ref}" />. Lab 3
    showed that putting a 100 nF cap right next to the TMP36 cleaned
    up about a degree of jitter on the scope; oversampling and
    averaging cleaned up the residual. The lesson generalises:
    component datasheet accuracy plus power-rail integrity dominate
    the error budget long before you start worrying about
    sub-millivolt ADC noise.
  </p>
</FeaturedProblem>
